Group 1. Kinds of evidence and their grades

A title says what kind of evidence it rests on, and every word, line and mark about that evidence follows the grade our systems atlas gives it.

Rule 13.1 Title each chart or diagram as a finding, an association, an estimate or an illustration.

The rule as the chapter states it

An evidence label, a chip in the colors our charts give their chips, sits above every chart title and names the kind of evidence in one of four words. A finding is a measured result with a source and a period, such as a count or a survey figure. An association is what our systems atlas defines as “Two measures that move together without an established causal path.” An estimate rests on a model’s assumptions, such as a projection. An illustration shows how a design works with invented values.

The grade follows the label in words: the atlas’s grade for a figure from the atlas (Rule 13.2), and Documented for a public agency’s own count or survey or a public record, with its source after the colon. A diagram of atlas connections prints the grade word of its least certain step in the label’s place.

Rule 13.2 Give the title the verb its evidence grade allows.

The rule as the chapter states it

House rule H08 in chapter 02 gives each evidence grade its verb: record words such as “passed” or “stood at” for documented, a direct verb such as “raises” for well supported, “can” or “may” for plausible, both readings at the same length for contested, and a label that marks a hypothesis as untested. A title takes the grade of the claim it states, read from the atlas record or set by Rule 13.1 for a figure from outside the atlas.

The atlas names each connection, a claimed causal link from one of its variables to another, and some names keep a direct verb at a lower grade, such as “Better public services build earned trust”, graded plausible. We print such a name in quotation marks after its typed chip, as the atlas writes it, and write our own title in the verb the grade allows.

Rule 13.3 Print the atlas’s grade words with a plain gloss, from one table.

The rule as the chapter states it

Our systems atlas stores its grades in several fields and spells some of them two ways: well-supported on a connection and well_supported on an issue record, plausible_contested on a connection and contested on a claim. Every page prints one display word for each grade, from the table in this rule, with a short gloss in a chart key and the atlas’s own definition the first time a page uses the word. A gloss is a plain phrase that says what the word means, such as “some evidence” for Plausible.

Two more words sit in the table. Mixed, for a claim whose parts point different ways, prints as the atlas words it, beside the record it describes. Retired marks an issue record the atlas no longer uses, which is a status, so it stays out of every grade key.

Rule 13.4 Draw a connection’s grade in the atlas’s line code.

The rule as the chapter states it

A connection, the arrow from one variable to another in our systems atlas, takes the line of its own grade, drawn by the same code that draws our systems atlas, as Rule 10.10 sets out: solid for documented and well supported, dashed for plausible, dash and dot for contested, and dots for a hypothesis. Its grade word prints beside it, and a documented connection’s label starts with the record square, a filled square that says the public record itself shows the link.

A loop, a chain of connections that returns to where it started, draws each step in its own line, so its least certain step stands out, and each step of a conceptual loop, one with no complete chain of numbered connections, draws as a hollow line. The oa.chart.grade tokens hold every number, listed in the Specifications.

Rule 13.5 Keep grade, effect size, uncertainty and delay apart, each with its own mark.

The rule as the chapter states it

Four properties of a claim keep their own marks and words. Two sit on the connection itself: the evidence grade, as the line of Rule 13.4 or the grade word, and delay, the time an effect takes, as ticks. A reform’s effect size prints as the atlas’s ordinal code, a rank of small, moderate or large with no measured amount, in one row for each outcome family, one of the four groups of results the atlas judges reforms by. For uncertainty we quote the atlas’s own words, or give a survey figure’s margin of error, the range around the figure that sampling allows.

Each mark carries one property, and each outcome family keeps its own row, as our systems atlas explains: “The four outcome families use different units. A reform can improve one while imposing a cost on another, and the evidence behind each estimate differs in quality.”

Group 2. Sources, dates and labels

A reader can trace every number to its publisher and its date, and can tell what we invented from what we found.

Rule 13.6 Write the source line in its six parts, in order.

The rule as the chapter states it

House rule H04 in chapter 02 sets the source line’s six parts and their order: the publisher, the title or table, the observation period, the geography, the date retrieved, and the qualification, the one caveat a reader most needs. The observation period is the time the numbers describe, and the date retrieved is the day we read the file. A chart prints the line under its notes, starting “Source:”, and a chart with two sources gives each its six parts.

A copy, a secondary source, an advocacy group and a value from our systems atlas each add a few words, which the Specifications set out.

Rule 13.7 Print the period and the vintage, and give charts shown together one window.

The rule as the chapter states it

The measure line names the observation period, such as 2009 to 2025. Where a publisher revises its figures, the chart also names the vintage, the release its figures come from, and marks any figure the publisher calls preliminary. The Census Bureau’s release of September 15, 2026 carries corrected poverty thresholds for 2019 to 2024 and new population weights for 2024, the counts that scale the survey’s sample to the country, so a chart of those years names that release.

Charts shown together share one time window, printed once with its reason, and house rule H09 holds: “No chart or sentence splits a period at an inauguration date.” When a publisher revises a figure a published chart shows, the chart takes the new release and a dated update line that names it; a correction note (Rule 13.13) is for our own errors.

Rule 13.8 Label an invented item Illustrative, beside the item.

The rule as the chapter states it

House rule H03 puts the word Illustrative beside every invented person, quote, place or figure a reader could take for real. A chart drawn from invented numbers carries “Illustrative data” beside its top edge, where a reader sees it before the numbers. When a piece mixes real and invented items, each invented item carries its own label and the real ones keep their sources. When every item in a piece is invented, one label at its head covers them all.

An invented bill takes a number from the ranges Rule 11.1 sets, from H.99001 and S.99002 up, which no legislature assigns, and an invented place takes a name we keep for examples, such as Alder County.

Rule 13.9 Name every atlas ID in its typed chip.

The rule as the chapter states it

Our systems atlas gives each record a short ID, and some IDs repeat across kinds of record. The code B4 names both Variable B4 and Loop B4, B2, B3 and B5 repeat the same way, and Loop R1 sits one hyphen from Reform R-01. Every atlas ID we print sits in a typed chip, the outlined label that holds the kind of record and its ID together, such as Variable B4 or Loop B4, built as Rule 18.24 sets out. The first time a page names a record, its name follows the chip, as house rule H11 asks.

The kind word comes from the record’s place in the atlas, and the build checks every chip’s ID against the atlas before a page ships. In a source line, the kind word in plain text does the chip’s job, as in “connection L02”.

Rule 13.10 Date what can change, and print an undated route as not checked.

The rule as the chapter states it

A status, a count or a rule that can change after we publish carries the date it was last checked, in words, as in “as of October 15, 2026”. Our systems atlas dates its research as a snapshot, and its glossary defines one as “The dated state of the research behind the Atlas, most recently October 15, 2026.” Each fact the atlas rechecked also carries a date of its own. A page prints a fact’s own check date where it has one, and the snapshot date where it does not.

A route, one of the paths a reform can take to become law or practice, carries its check date when the atlas gives one and the words “Not checked” when it does not. Of the atlas’s 126 route entries, 13 carry a date. When something the record names has happened since its date, such as an election, the page keeps the dated words and adds what happened, with its own source and date.

Group 3. Records, quotes and corrections

Dated events, quoted words and our own corrections each take a form a reader can check against the record.

Rule 13.11 Draw documented events apart from causal claims.

The rule as the chapter states it

Our systems atlas keeps two logs of dated events. The repair log records 64 changes to the rules that decide who officials answer to, and the delivery log records 24 times government did something hard at scale with a measured result. An event records what happened on a date; a causal claim, which the atlas grades, says that one thing moves another.

On a chart or diagram, an event takes a mark of its own, a shape for its instrument with an outline for one still pending, and no arrowhead, line pattern or sign. Where the atlas codes an event’s direction, such as toward voters, the page prints the atlas’s words and names the atlas as their source, apart from the event’s date, place and text. A causal claim keeps the arrow, the sign and the line of Rule 13.4.

Rule 13.12 Quote a person exactly, with who, when and where.

The rule as the chapter states it

A quotation prints a person’s words exactly as the record holds them, set in Lora Italic as Rule 06.1 sets, and then says who said them, the role they held at the time, when, and where: the hearing, the ruling, the speech or the interview. A source line gives the record a reader can open. Words we add for sense sit in square brackets, and an ellipsis marks a cut only where the meaning stays the same.

In a chart or diagram, a quote sits beside the mark it speaks to as a note with its attribution, and the title stays our sentence about the evidence (Rule 13.1). An Act piece quotes each side at the same length (Rule 01.22), and an invented quote carries the Illustrative label beside it (Rule 13.8).

Rule 13.13 Correct in the open, with a dated note at the top.

The rule as the chapter states it

When a piece we published gets a fact, a figure or a chart wrong, we fix it and say so at its top. A correction note sits above the headline with the date of the fix and says what was wrong and what changed, in plain sentences. The fixed chart replaces the old one in the piece, so every reader and every share meets the right figures, and the note says what the old chart showed.

The same note goes on our corrections page, newest first, with the piece’s headline, a link to it and the date it first ran, and both stay as long as the piece is published. A correction takes no red: its words take the primary text color and its 4 px bar the essential graphic color, as Rule 26.3’s example sets out.
