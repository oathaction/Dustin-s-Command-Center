1 · Positioning and the brand idea

The ampersand is the hinge, and the hinge turns in a loop

Recommendation. Make the hinge the organizing idea of the whole system Option. Your idea (DC-05) is a promise or commitment, then action. Each locked line already has that shape.

Positioning

For people in every state who live under public rules they rarely get to read, Oath & Action is a nonpartisan publication and set of tools. It explains how a decision is made and gives a practical way to take part. Readers can check it: claims carry a source and an evidence grade, and every explanation ends with a step.

Points of difference

  • It explains the path from a rule to its result, with evidence graded the way the atlas grades it.
  • Every piece turns at the hinge divider to "What you can do."
  • Readers of every party and state get the same record and the same way in.
  • A position piece carries a label before its headline and the strongest case against it.

The hinge drawn as a loop

Your mockups circle the ampersand with two dashed arcs, each with arrowheads at both ends, so the figure reads as an exchange. When both arcs run one way it becomes a loop, which Atlas V7's glossary defines as "A chain of causes that returns to its starting variable." V5's own hinge arc is dotted, and the atlas draws hypotheses dotted and plausible links dashed. So the proposal uses solid arcs.

What this lets the brand say, and what it must not claim

Loop R9 has the brand's shape. Loop B4, "Electoral consequences can improve public performance," is what V7 calls "The main balancing loop in the model," well-supported where seats are competitive. It describes the condition the tagline names.

The brand may present its loop as a goal and a design. Any claim about its effect carries R9's grade and uses "can" or "may" (Master Brief section 15).

Brand narrative (232 words)

Every public rule starts with people who promised to serve: a council that adopts a budget, a legislature that passes a bill. Then the rule reaches a household as a rent renewal, a pay stub, a ballot or a power bill.

Oath & Action follows that path for readers in every state. We read the rule and show who holds the decision. The record stays within reach while the decision moves. When it is time to act, we point to the office that answers for it and the step a reader can take.

The ampersand in our name is the hinge between a promise and the action that keeps it. It joins a rule to its result and a problem to the fixes on the table. It also joins people: you and a neighbor who lives under the same rule and may vote another way. You both get the same record and the same way in.

The hinge turns in a loop. People who take part and see the result can hold the next promise to account, and the people who made it answer to them. Our tagline names that goal: "A country that answers to its people."

We are nonpartisan. When credible sources disagree, we show both readings and the evidence for each. When we take a position, we label it before the headline and summarize the strongest case against it.

Naming rules

  1. Names describe what the product does. Only the atlas is titled as a question.
  2. No product name contains an ampersand. A partner's ampersand is written "and" (V5).
  3. Every piece carries a content label: Explainer, Tracker, Analysis, Report, Act: How to take part, or Act: Our position.
  4. Keep V5's series names. "Dispatch from the Front" becomes "Dispatch," which V5's kicker already uses Option.
  5. Drop "scorecards" from Track. Grading individual officials is an advocacy format.
  6. An atlas reform is an option studied, with its grade. Positions live only in labeled Act pieces.

"From spectators to stewards."

Recommended role: none as a brand line. Record it in Purpose as the founding line.

  • V4.2 lists it as "Historical tagline" and "Retired from current applications." Your slides return it to the tagline slot (p01, p07, p13, square poster), where it competes with "A country that answers to its people."
  • The "from X to Y" turn is a contrasting pair, which the voice rules exclude.
  • It calls the newcomer a spectator, which scolds the reader we most want to welcome.
  • It favors no party, and stewardship has a home in faith, conservation and fiscal traditions alike (design judgment).
  • The narrowest role, if you want one: membership and events, never in a lockup. The research lead checks prior use first.

"Democracy has a structure."

Recommended role: the lockmark's explanatory line, in the Signal Grid chapter and on the About page, followed by a sentence that names the lever.

  • Plain and present tense, and it names a mechanism. It passes the voice rules.
  • It restates V5: the lockmark "says that democracy has a locatable structure and that one point moves it."
  • Campaigns in both parties have made "democracy" a theme, and some readers answer it with "a republic." The risk is low in an explanatory role. Studio and editor judgment, untested with readers.
  • Keep it out of the tagline slot. Fallback: "Every public decision has a structure."

Where the parts sit

1 The kicker, above the headline. "Our position" is a filled Charcoal label, read first. Red stays on the ask button.

2 Both readings. In a position piece, the strongest opposing case comes before the ask.

3 The disclosure line, directly above the ask button in a position piece.

4 Who decided, when, funder interest, and the position log.

What keeps a position piece fair

  1. The label leads, and the disclosure repeats above the ask. On a shared card the label holds the top-left corner.
  2. The strongest case against is written as its supporters would put it, with sources, at no less than a third of the argument's length.
  3. Every claim keeps its evidence grade, with a verb to match.
  4. Opponents are named by office or role, never as a party villain.
  5. Process facts match the neutral tracker word for word.
  6. A public position log lists every position by date and issue.
  7. Positions never appear in the atlas, trackers or data.

6 · Nonpartisan rule set

Twenty rules that keep every piece readable by every reader

Recommendation. Adopt NP-01 to NP-20 Option. The NP prefix cannot collide with atlas or Standards IDs.

Color

  1. NP-01 Pillar Red and Prussian never share a figure. Prussian surfaces carry Newsprint marks. The locked lockups on Prussian go to the identity board.
  2. NP-02 Two sides never take red against any blue or against Verdigris. Use Charcoal and Stone, or solid and outline, with direct labels.
  3. NP-03 Diverging scales center on Newsprint, use two hues outside the red and blue pair, and name both directions in words.
  4. NP-04 Red has one job per layout and never marks a party, side or person.
  5. NP-05 Maps never color places by party or result.

Imagery

  1. NP-06 Domes, columns and flags appear only in a captioned photograph of that place or event.
  2. NP-07 No party symbols, candidate photos, campaign merchandise or legible protest slogans. A party logo may appear as evidence in a sourced document.
  3. NP-08 Photographs show people of different ages, places and roles at public work, officials included, captioned who, what, where and when.

Rotation

  1. NP-09 In a chapter, no V7 domain supplies more than one example in four, and no state more than one in five.
  2. NP-10 Rotate federal, state, county or city and school district decisions, and regions.
  3. NP-11 When majorities of either party use a mechanism, show both. Loop R4a, "Majorities draw maps that help them keep control," is the test case.
  4. NP-12 A rotation log records domain, state, level and the coalition that usually backs any reform shown, reviewed at each gate.

Language

  1. NP-13 Name the office that decides. Name a party only when party is the mechanism, with the count and source.
  2. NP-14 No grievance framing: "the rule you are angry about," "rigged," "broken," "crisis."
  3. NP-15 No war or sports metaphors: fight, battle, front.
  4. NP-16 Contested claims show both readings at equal length, with the same verbs and type.
  5. NP-17 A reader who supports the current rule would accept its description.

Hinge pairs

  1. NP-18 Both halves share one color and weight. Only the ampersand is red.
  2. NP-19 People pairs join people through a shared rule or place, and their order rotates.
  3. NP-20 "Left," "right" or party names enter a hinge headline only to report a sourced agreement.

7 · Art direction

Take the contrast of heavy capitals and a serif ampersand, and leave the domes

What the slides share. Newsprint grounds, heavy condensed capitals, one red serif ampersand, the Signal Grid at bottom right, Prussian panels and black-and-white scenes of people. They repeat: p07 appears five times (p07, p37, p38, p43, p46), and nine groups of pages are duplicates.

Condensed heavy wordmark

p01, p07, p08, p13, p40, p41, banner

Take heavy grotesque capitals against a calligraphic ampersand. Two kinds of letter joined is the hinge made visible. The square poster departs, with a wide sans.

Avoid condensed capitals in every headline (p17, p40) and the substitute face named on p09.

Serif red ampersand

p07 sections 05 to 07, p15, p41

Take circle and square containers in one size system.

Avoid the ampersand in Verdigris, Stone or Prussian (p07); seals with a ring of words (p07, p41) or "EST. 2026" (p03, p25). V5: "Seals imply an authority we do not claim."

Hinge circle

p04, p07, p12, p41, both mockups

Take the circle around the ampersand and the two pin dots where the line meets it. It can become a wordless badge for stickers and merchandise.

Avoid double arrowheads, dashed arcs and labels under 13 px.

Pillar badges

p01, p04, p08, p40, p45

Take four filled circles in pillar order as a navigation row.

Avoid the ampersand and monogram reused as pillar icons, Prussian and red in one row, and the names "Understand," "Expose" and "Strengthen" (p01, p03, p17).

Signal Grid as pattern

p15, square poster

Take the node-and-link field at low density on covers, merchandise and openers Option.

Avoid lit red or Verdigris nodes in a pattern, pattern behind body text, and a 5 by 5 lockmark.

Documentary photography

p01, p05, p15, p17, p40

Take people doing the work, in natural light, in black and white, with captions that name the act.

Avoid treating any of these as photographs: all are generated. Also flags and "VOTE" signs as props (p05), legible protest signs (p01, p05) and buildings without people (p03).

Editorial social templates

p01, p02, p17, p40, p41

Take headline top left, wordmark bottom left, grid bottom right, numbered carousels (p41).

Avoid unsourced numbers such as "12.4M" (p01, p04, p17, p41), slogan triplets (p17, p40), all-red tiles (p40, p41) and quotes credited to invented groups (p17).

Data-led cards

p14 above all, p17

Take from p14: a sentence title, units in the subtitle, the illustrative label inside each chart, direct labels, red only on the point the title names.

Avoid icon-bullet percentages with a vague source (p17) and a growth line called a civic impact index (p41).

Clichés to avoid

The Capitol dome and columns (p03, p08, p24, p25, p41). Scales of justice, stars, shields, a gold-on-navy heritage plaque and a rising-sun pediment (p25). The U.S. map silhouette and the handshake (p25). Megaphones (p15, p40). Flags behind type (p25). The phrases "Democracy is not a spectator sport" (p25) and "Be the change" (p17).

Until a photography decision is made (Q-04), use labeled placeholders that announce themselves as placeholders.
