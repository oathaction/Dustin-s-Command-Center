CHAPTER 02 · VOICE

Why we write this way

Our tagline is “A country that answers to its people.” People can hold an office to account once they know which office decided. So we name who acts, in words you would use at a kitchen table, as in “A regulator or a board approves your rate.”

Our reader promise is “We explain how a decision is made and give you a practical way to take part.” A reader can take part only in a decision they can follow, which is why clarity comes first in our editorial commitment, “Clarity is our duty. Action is our promise.”

Our founder and editor, Dustin Cathcart, set these rules for everything Oath & Action publishes. Sixteen of the 21 rules quote our own work and rewrite the line to pass every rule on the page. Where we found none, we wrote one, and anything we invented carries the Illustrative label.

01

Sound like a person talking

Write the sentence you would say across a kitchen table, with the actor up front and a rhythm that keeps changing.

W01

Write it the way you would say it

Write the way you would explain something to a neighbor, in everyday words and full sentences. Read the draft aloud and rewrite any line you would not say.

Plain prose: everyday words in complete sentences, with nothing a listener would need to see on the page to follow.

What changed. The rewrite names the office, the thing a reader would ask about, and read aloud it sounds like one person talking to another.

becomes
W02

Name who acts

Put the person or office that acts in front of the verb. When you find a passive verb, ask who does the action and name them.

Active voice: a sentence that names the actor first, as in “The council passed the budget.” Passive voice puts the thing acted on first and often hides the actor, as in “The budget was passed.”

What changed. The rewrite names who decides in its first five words, so a listener hears the answer before anything else.

becomes
W03

Change the length of your sentences

Mix short sentences with long ones, and keep the mix hard to predict. A run of sentences the same size starts to sound like a metronome.

What changed. The rewrite defines consent orders in one long sentence ahead of two shorter ones, so the voice rises and falls and a newcomer can follow.

becomes
W04

Join thoughts that belong together

Use the small words that show how thoughts connect, such as because, so, when and once. Save short sentences for the moments that need one.

Parataxis: setting clauses or sentences side by side with no words to link them, which as a rhythm device chops one thought into short beats.

What changed. “Once” tells a listener that finding the lever depends on knowing the rules, and the rewrite defines the lever in the atlas glossary’s words.

becomes
02

Put the point first and stop

Open with what the reader came for, and end when the facts run out.

Dotted underline: what the rule removesSolid underline: the repairIllustrative: written to show the fault, not quoted

W05

Open with something the reader can use

Make the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step. Cut lines that only announce what comes next, and stock phrases that lean on the words after them.

Throat-clearing opener: a sentence that announces what is coming in place of saying it. Rhetorical crutch: a stock phrase that props up a sentence and adds no meaning, such as “the truth is” or “make no mistake”.

What changed. Read aloud, the rewrite gives the deadline and both ways to take part, which “here is how” only promised.

becomes
W06

Give the point without a reveal

Put the point where the reader meets it first. A colon or a teaser sentence that holds it back for effect only makes the reader wait.

Setup and payoff: holding back the point so it lands as a reveal, often after a colon or a teaser sentence.

What changed. The rewrite gives the answer in the first breath and runs two words shorter.

becomes
W07

Stop when the facts stop

End a paragraph on its last useful fact, and cut any line after it that only restates the point.

Landing sentence: a closing line written to make a paragraph land with a flourish. Paragraph pinning: ending paragraph after paragraph on a short line that restates its point. Summary beat: a sentence that tells readers what they have read.

What changed. The rewrite states the point once, at the start, so a listener hears the paragraph end on its last new fact.

becomes
W08

Close without a slogan

End a piece or section on the last thing the reader needs, usually a step with its date. Where a sign-off line belongs, use the locked tagline.

Slogan-like closing sentence: a short, quotable line at the end of a piece that sounds like an advertisement and carries no fact.

What changed. The rewrite ends on a step and its date, so the last words a listener hears say what to do.

becomes
03

Say what is so

State what is true in plain sentences, and leave out the shapes writers use for effect.

Dotted underline: what the rule removesSolid underline: the repairIllustrative: written to show the fault, not quoted

W09

Say what a thing is

Tell the reader what something is. Leave out the turn that first says what it is not, and the phrases set against each other for effect.

Corrective negation: saying what something is not before saying what it is, as in “X, not Y” or “not X but Y”. Antithesis: two opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

What changed. The rewrite tells a photographer what to shoot, and a listener hears no “not” to undo.

becomes
W10

Drop the matched pair

Write the relation between two things as a plain sentence. Two short halves set side by side sound like a slogan and leave the reader to supply the link.

Contrasting pair: two short matched phrases placed side by side for their rhythm. A hinge headline such as “The zoning code & the rent.” stays, since it joins a rule to its result under its own rules in chapter 04.

What changed. The rewrite makes the comparison in one sentence, so a listener hears no echo of “counts” in the second half.

becomes
W11

Skip the run of negatives

When you rule several things out, put them in one list under one verb and give the reason.

Negative parallelism: a run of matched negatives, such as “no this, no that”. Negative anaphora: starting clause after clause with the same negative word.

What changed. One verb carries the whole list in the rewrite, and a listener hears the reason with it.

becomes
W12

List what the facts hold

Give a list as many items as the facts hold. When there are three, vary their shape so they do not fall into a chant.

Rule of three, or rhetorical triplet: grouping words or phrases in threes because three sounds finished. A plain list of three is fine when the facts have three parts.

What changed. The rewrite turns the chant into one instruction a first-time speaker can follow, and read aloud it sounds like advice.

becomes
W13

Give each sentence its own shape

Build each sentence in a paragraph on its own frame. When several in a row open the same way, combine them or recast one.

Parallel structure: sentences built on the same frame one after another, such as a run that each starts with “We”.

What changed. Four sentences on one frame become two of different lengths in the rewrite, the voice line we now use, which drops “We expose the gaps” and hands the last step to the reader on purpose. Our editorial commitment keeps its two-part shape, since locked lines keep their wording (H01).

becomes
04

Cut the padding

Take out the words that add weight and no meaning.

Dotted underline: what the rule removesSolid underline: the repairIllustrative: written to show the fault, not quoted

W14

Pull stacked nouns apart

When three or more nouns pile up, pull them apart with a preposition or a verb, so a listener can find the main noun.

Stacked noun phrase: three or more nouns in a row, each describing the next, with the main noun last.

What changed. Read aloud, “report cover kicker” runs together, and the rewrite puts the main noun first and says where it sits.

becomes
W15

Use the verb inside the noun

When a noun hides a verb, use the verb and name who does it.

Nominalization: a verb or adjective turned into a noun, such as “allocation” from “allocate” or “implementation” from “implement”.

What changed. The rewrite brings back the verbs, so a listener hears who sets money aside and what the amount cannot show.

becomes
W16

Drop the hedges and keep the grade

State a claim at the strength its evidence holds. When the evidence grade calls for “may” or “can”, use that word once and cut the other softeners.

Hedging qualifier: a word that softens a claim with no reason given, such as “perhaps” or “somewhat”. Grade verbs work differently: for a claim graded plausible, “may” or “can” reports the grade, as H08 requires.

What changed. The rewrite lets one grade verb carry the doubt the evidence holds and names the grade, and read aloud it is one plain claim.

becomes
W17

Cut the intensifiers

Cut words that try to add force, such as genuinely, really, truly, actually, simply and just. If the sentence feels weak without one, give it a stronger fact.

Filler intensifier: a word meant to add emphasis that adds no information.

What changed. The rewrite drops “really” and adds that the vote comes the same night, which gives a listener the urgency the word only signaled.

becomes
W18

Use everyday verbs

Pick the word you would say out loud. Write “use” for leverage, and “show” for underscore or reflect.

Corporate register: the vocabulary of business memos. The atlas glossary’s “leverage point”, a systems term from Donella Meadows, is a defined term and stays.

What changed. The rewrite uses the words a resident would say at a council meeting, and “more homes” tells a listener who gains.

becomes
W19

Let the facts carry the feeling

Leave out exclamation points and cheering, and let the step itself carry the energy.

Performed enthusiasm: excitement acted out on the page in place of a reason to care.

What changed. The rewrite names whom to tell and when, and read aloud it sounds like advice from a neighbor.

becomes
W20

Size each word to its evidence

Give an adjective or a label no more weight than its evidence carries, and when we praise our own work, label the praise as a design judgment.

Inflated adjective: a word that claims more than its evidence, such as “stunning”, or a name wider than what was measured.

What changed. The survey counts citizens 18 and over, so the rewrite uses those words, and its margin-of-error test makes the title say only what a listener could check on the chart.

becomes
05

Marks and numbers

Set dashes, dates, numbers and signs the same way on every page.

Dotted underline: what the rule removesSolid underline: the repairIllustrative: written to show the fault, not quoted

W21

Leave out the em dash

Where you want an em dash, end the sentence, or join the parts with a comma and a word such as “and” or “because”.

Em dash: the long dash, as wide as a capital M, that writers use to break into a sentence or tack a thought onto its end.

What changed. The rewrite’s comma and “and” tell a listener that the vote follows the deadline.

BEFORE YOU PUBLISH

Read it aloud, then ask

  1. Could you say each sentence aloud to a neighbor, with the person who acts named first?
  2. Do your sentences change in length and shape as the paragraph goes on?
  3. Does the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step, and does the last one end on a fact?
  4. Does each sentence say what something is, with no matched pair or “not this, but that” turn?
  5. Have you turned every noun that hides a verb back into its verb, and pulled apart every stack of nouns?
  6. Does each claim sit at the strength its evidence grade allows, with no word added for force or caution?
  7. Are the locked lines exact, and does every invented line, person, place or figure carry the Illustrative label?
  8. Does every public policy statistic carry its full source line?
  9. Are parties named in words, in the same ink as the text, and is each contested policy called by its neutral name?
  10. Are months written as words, numbers under ten spelled out, minus signs true and em dashes gone?

Decisions on this board

  1. Do the locked lines keep their wording where a rule would change them? Our editorial commitment, “Clarity is our duty. Action is our promise.”, is two sentences on one frame, the shape W13 asks writers to avoid, and the tagline is a phrase. H01 keeps their wording, and W13’s note says so where a reader meets the clash.Recommended: Confirm the exemption.
  2. Do hinge headlines stay? A hinge headline such as “The zoning code & the rent.” joins two noun phrases by design, the shape W10 removes elsewhere. W10’s terms line keeps it, since it joins a rule to its result.Recommended: Confirm the exemption.
  3. Is voice line B the right rewrite for W13? W13’s rewrite is the voice line you approved at Gate 1. It drops “We expose the gaps” and hands the last step to the reader, so it is the one rewrite that changes meaning on purpose, and its note says so.Recommended: Keep voice line B.
  4. Retire the four V5 lines that fail these rules? “Know the rules. Find the lever.”, “Institutions endure when people act.”, “The fine print affects the big picture.” and “Bring your questions. They belong here.” each fail a rule here. The locked lines and voice line B already do each of their jobs.Recommended: Retire all four, with no role.
  5. Keep 21 rules in five groups? Each rule carries its own worked example. The rules now sit in five teaching groups, with an index of every rule name after the opening.Recommended: Keep 21 rules in the five groups.
  6. How should W21 show the em dash? W21’s before now prints the real em dash, the only one the page allows. The board can also draw it as a rule one em wide, labeled for screen readers.Recommended: Show it, drawn one em wide and labeled “em dash” for screen readers.
  7. Which studio drafts stay as befores? W05 keeps a civic mock from an early draft of this guide. The other three studio befores came from internal boards, and lines a public reader can check now stand in W07, W10 and W20.Recommended: Keep W05 and the three swaps.

  10. Do “may” and “can” stay in W16? W16 tells writers that “may” and “can” report a plausible grade, as H08 says, so they stay when the evidence calls for them.Recommended: Confirm that reading.

HOW THIS WAS MADE

The editor wrote the rules and the creative director tested them

  1. You approved your own writing rules as Oath & Action's public rules and asked to see them before and after (DR-70).
  2. The editor wrote the rules from your standing instructions and the studio's house rules, and searched the committed files for real Oath & Action copy to use as befores. A script found every real before word for word in its named source.
  3. The creative director read every after against every rule on the page, checked each real before by script, rendered the draft board at desktop and phone width, and scored the section 3 on six lines and 4 on usefulness.
  4. The editor made one fix pass: rewritten afters, civic befores in place of three studio drafts, five teaching groups, marked edits and source lines in public words.
  5. The main session redesigned the board from the review: each before joined to its after by the hinge divider, the edit marked in both, a rule index, larger specimens in Inter, and a phone layout. It built and checked the board and published it as a private page.
