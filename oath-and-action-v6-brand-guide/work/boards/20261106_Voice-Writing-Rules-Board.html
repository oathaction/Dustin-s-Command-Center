    CHAPTER 02 · VOICE

    Why we write this way

    Our reader promise is “We explain how a decision is made and give you a practical way to take part.” A reader can take part only in a decision they can follow, and that puts the first half of the promise on our writing.

    Our editorial commitment reads, “Clarity is our duty. Action is our promise.” These rules carry out the first of those two sentences. Our founder and editor, Dustin Cathcart, set them as his standing instructions, and they now govern everything Oath & Action publishes. Each asks for sentences you could say aloud to a neighbor.

    Nearly every rule below quotes a sentence we published or drafted, with its source. We wrote the other two ourselves and labeled them Illustrative, the label every invented place or date carries. Beside each, we rewrite the same facts to pass every rule on the page.

    GROUP 1 OF 4

    Sound like a person talking

    Write the sentence you would say across a kitchen table, with the actor up front and a rhythm that keeps changing.

    W01

    Write it the way you would say it

    Write the way you would explain something to a neighbor, in everyday words and full sentences. Read the draft aloud and rewrite any line you would not say.

    In plain words: Plain prose: everyday words in complete sentences, with nothing a listener would need to see on the page to follow.

    What changed. Nobody says “the point of responsibility” out loud, and “which office” is the question a reader would ask.

    W02

    Name who acts

    Put the person or office that acts in front of the verb. When you find a passive verb, ask who does the action and name them.

    In plain words: Active voice names the actor first: “The council passed the budget.” Passive voice puts the thing acted on first and often hides the actor: “The budget was passed.”

    What changed. The after names who decides in its first five words, so a listener hears the answer before anything else.

    W03

    Change the length of your sentences

    Mix short sentences with long ones, and keep the mix hard to predict. A run of sentences the same size starts to sound like a metronome.

    What changed. The steps keep their order, and a three-word opener followed by one long sentence lets the voice rise and fall.

    GROUP 2 OF 4

    Say it straight

    Say what is so in plain order, and start with what the reader came for.

    W04

    Leave out the em dash

    Where you want an em dash, end the sentence, or join the parts with a comma and a word such as “and” or “because”.

    In plain words: Em dash: the long dash, as wide as a capital M, that writers use to break into a sentence or tack a thought onto its end.

    What changed. The comma and “and” tell a listener that the vote follows the deadline.

    W05

    Say what a thing is

    Tell the reader what something is. Leave out the turn that first says what it is not, and the phrases set against each other for effect.

    In plain words: Corrective negation: saying what something is not before saying what it is, as in “X, not Y” or “not X but Y”. Antithesis: two opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

    What changed. A photographer can act on the after at once, with no need to guess what “the idea of it” rules out.

    W06

    Drop the matched pair

    Write the relation between two things as a plain sentence. Two short halves set side by side sound like a slogan and leave the reader to supply the link.

    In plain words: Contrasting pair: two short matched phrases placed side by side for their rhythm. A hinge headline, which joins two noun phrases with the ampersand, is a named format with its own rules in chapter 04 and stays.

    What changed. The after says in one breath what the table under it shows, so the heading stops sounding like a tagline.

    W07

    Skip the run of negatives

    When you rule several things out, put them in one list under one verb and give the reason.

    In plain words: Negative parallelism: a run of matched negatives, such as “no this, no that”. Negative anaphora: starting clause after clause with the same negative word.

    What changed. One verb now carries the whole list, and the reason comes with it.

    W08

    Open with something the reader can use

    Make the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step. Cut lines that only announce what comes next, and stock phrases that lean on the words after them.

    In plain words: Throat-clearing opener: a sentence that announces what is coming in place of saying it. Rhetorical crutch: a stock phrase that props up a sentence and adds no meaning, such as “the truth is” or “make no mistake”.

    What changed. The second sentence now gives both ways to take part and the comment deadline, which “here is how” only promised.

    GROUP 3 OF 4

    Let the sentence carry its own weight

    Trust the facts to hold a reader, and drop the patterns writers build to make prose sound rhythmic.

    W09

    List what the facts hold

    Give a list as many items as the facts hold. When there are three, vary their shape so they do not fall into a chant.

    In plain words: Rule of three, or rhetorical triplet: grouping words or phrases in threes because three sounds finished. A plain list of three is fine when the facts have three parts.

    What changed. The same parts arrive as one instruction a first-time speaker can follow, with no chant to remember.

    W10

    Give each sentence its own shape

    Build each sentence in a paragraph on its own frame. When several in a row open the same way, combine them or recast one.

    In plain words: Parallel structure: sentences built on the same frame one after another, such as a run that each starts with “We”.

    What changed. Four sentences on one frame became two of different lengths. This is the voice line now in force, and it gives the last step to the reader on purpose.

    W11

    Join thoughts that belong together

    Use the small words that show how thoughts connect, such as because, so, when and once. Save short sentences for the moments that need one.

    In plain words: Parataxis: setting clauses or sentences side by side with no words to link them. As a rhythm device, it chops one thought into short beats for effect.

    What changed. “Once” shows that finding the lever depends on knowing the rule, and the lever gets its definition.

    W12

    Give the point without a reveal

    Put the point where the reader meets it first. A colon or a teaser sentence that holds it back for effect only makes the reader wait.

    In plain words: Setup and payoff: holding back the point so it lands as a reveal, often after a colon or a teaser sentence.

    What changed. The answer arrives in the first breath, and the sentence loses two words.

    W13

    Stop when the facts stop

    End a paragraph on its last useful fact, and cut any line after it that only restates the point.

    In plain words: Landing sentence: a closing line written to make a paragraph land with a flourish. Paragraph pinning: ending paragraph after paragraph on a short line that restates its point. Summary beat: a sentence that tells readers what they have read.

    What changed. The after defines the node and shows the link that the “so” clause only claimed.

    W14

    Close without a slogan

    End a piece or section on the last thing the reader needs, usually the step they can take. Where a sign-off line belongs, use the locked tagline.

    In plain words: Slogan-like closing sentence: a short, quotable line at the end of a piece that sounds like an advertisement and carries no fact.

    What changed. The after tells the reader what the closing page holds, which the slogan never said.

    GROUP 4 OF 4

    Cut the padding

    Take out the words that add weight and no meaning.

    W15

    Pull stacked nouns apart

    When three or more nouns pile up, pull them apart with a preposition or a verb, so a listener can tell which word is the subject.

    In plain words: Stacked noun phrase: three or more nouns in a row, each describing the next.

    What changed. Read aloud, “report cover kicker” blurs into one sound, and the after says what the kicker is and where it sits.

    W16

    Use the verb inside the noun

    When a noun hides a verb, use the verb and name who does it.

    In plain words: Nominalization: a verb or adjective turned into a noun, such as “allocation” from “allocate” or “implementation” from “implement”.

    What changed. With its verbs back, the sentence says who sets money aside and what the amount leaves unanswered.

    W17

    Drop the hedges and keep the grade

    State a claim at the strength its evidence holds. When the evidence grade calls for “may” or “can”, use that word once and cut the other softeners.

    In plain words: Hedging qualifier: a word that softens a claim with no reason given, such as “perhaps” or “somewhat”. Grade verbs work differently: for a claim graded plausible, “may” or “can” reports the grade, and the house rules require it.

    What changed. Four softeners said nothing exact. One grade verb now carries the doubt the evidence holds, and the second sentence names the grade.

    W18

    Cut the intensifiers

    Cut words that try to add force, such as genuinely, really, truly, actually, simply and just. If the sentence feels weak without one, give it a stronger fact.

    In plain words: Filler intensifier: a word meant to add emphasis that adds no information.

    What changed. “Simply” and “already” gave a listener nothing to check, and one sentence now says what appears.

    W19

    Use everyday verbs

    Pick the word you would say out loud. Write “use” for leverage, and “show” for underscore or reflect.

    In plain words: Corporate register: the vocabulary of business memos. The atlas glossary’s “leverage point”, a systems term from Donella Meadows, is a defined term and stays.

    What changed. “Leverage” is boardroom talk. The after says what the mark tells a reader, in the words V5’s data chapter used for the same mark.

    W20

    Let the facts carry the feeling

    Leave out exclamation points and praise for our own work, and let the step itself carry the energy.

    In plain words: Performed enthusiasm: excitement acted out on the page in place of a reason to care.

    What changed. The after names whom to tell and when, which is the part a reader can act on.

    W21

    Size each adjective to its evidence

    Give an adjective no more weight than its evidence carries, and label a design judgment as a judgment.

    In plain words: Inflated adjective: a word bigger than its evidence, such as “stunning” or “world-class”.

    What changed. The judgment carries its label, and “portfolio work” becomes a claim a reader can check against the page.

    HOUSE RULES

    The rules every page already follows

    RuleWhat it asksWritten correctly
    H01Quote the locked lines exactly
    Quote the tagline, the mission, the reader promise and the editorial commitment word for word, and keep the pillars in their order: Explain, Track, Connect, Act. The locked lines keep their wording even where a writing rule above would shape a sentence differently.    		"A country that answers to its people."
    H02Explain a term the first time you use it
    Define each term of art in plain words where it first appears, using the atlas glossary’s wording when it fits. Keep the same term after that.    		Cloture, a Senate vote to end debate, takes 60 votes for most legislation.
    H03Label what is invented beside it
    Put the word Illustrative beside every invented person, quote, place or figure a reader could take for real, right on the item.    		Alder County votes on backyard cottages March 9. Illustrative
    H04Source every public policy statistic
    Give the publisher, the title or table, the observation period, the geography, the date retrieved and the qualification that matters, in that order.    		Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey Voting and Registration Supplement, Table 4a, reported voting for states, November 2024, 50 states and DC. Retrieved November 6, 2026. Self-reported voting among citizens 18 and over; state margins of error reach 5.6 points.
    H05Write dates in words
    Write the month as a word, as in November 6, 2026. Numeric dates belong in file names and data.    		Retrieved November 6, 2026.
    H06Spell out numbers under ten
    In prose, write one to nine as words. Charts, tables, measurements and data use figures.    		payrolls shrank in seven of the 57 months charted
    H07Use the true minus sign
    Write a negative number with the minus sign (U+2212). It matches the plus sign’s width and lines up in tables, and a hyphen is too short to read as minus.    		The scale runs from −200,000 to +850,000.
    H08Let a title claim only what its evidence holds
    A title states only what its evidence shows, with the verb its grade allows: record language for documented claims, a direct finding when the evidence is well supported, “can” or “may” for plausible claims, a label marking a hypothesis as untested, and both readings when a claim is contested.    		The three-month average of monthly job change stood at 51,000 in September 2026, a preliminary figure, and payrolls shrank in seven of the 57 months charted.
    H09Keep a period whole
    No chart or sentence splits a period at an inauguration date. Charts shown together share one time window and print the reason for it.    		Both jobs charts cover January 2022 to September 2026, the same 57 months, so a reader compares like with like.
    H10Name parties in words
    Write a party’s name in words, in the same ink as the text around it. No election graphic, social cards included, codes a party with red or blue.    		Georgia Republican +2.2

    BEFORE YOU PUBLISH

    Read it aloud, then ask

    1. Could you say each sentence this way to a neighbor, with the person who acts named?
    2. Do your sentences change in length and shape as the paragraph goes on?
    3. Does a search for the em dash come back empty?
    4. Does the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step?
    5. Does each sentence say what something is, with no matched pair or “not this, but that” turn?
    6. If you cut the last line of a paragraph or a piece, would the reader lose a fact?
    7. Can any heavy noun give its verb back, or any stack of nouns come apart?
    8. Does each claim sit at the strength its evidence grade allows, with no word added for force or caution?
    9. Are the locked lines exact, and does every invented item carry the Illustrative label?
    10. Does every public policy statistic carry its full source line?
