    Decision 2: Ampersand B in a hinge headline, on Newsprint

    DD10-NF1; locked identity, so its own named approval (CLAUDE.md gate rule 4)

    In a hinge headline such as “The zoning code & the rent.”, B stands for the word “and” in a line a reader must read, so the accessibility lead rules it is text. Pillar Red on Newsprint measures 2.96 to 1, under the 3 to 1 that WCAG 2.2 sets for large text (criterion 1.4.3), and WCAG does not round. B in the wordmark, the lockmark, the favicon, the avatars and the badge names the brand and keeps WCAG’s logotype exception.

    The studio recommends: On Newsprint, give a hinge headline’s B Rule 05.3’s keyline, a 1 px Charcoal edge drawn inside the mark (13.29 to 1). B keeps its red, its shape and its size. On White (4.17 to 1) and Charcoal (4.49 to 1) B already passes and takes no keyline. The other way, B in the headline’s ink, needs no keyline and spends no red, and it changes Rule 04.3’s red moment.

    Decision 5: Chapter 18’s masthead code: the current part and the menu’s first row

    E11b-1 and E11b-2, approved chapter 18

    The masthead reads any aria-current attribute as the current part, including aria-current="false", which some page builders write on every other link, so a partner’s copy would draw every part as current and open the menu on the first (E11b-1). No page writes "false" today. In the open menu, the 2 px focus ring on the first row draws its top side under the title row’s 1 px rule (E11b-2); WCAG 2.4.11 is met, and the website’s own menu leaves a gap there.

    The studio recommends: Apply both as one change to chapter 18’s code set: match [aria-current]:not([aria-current="false"]), as the guide check already reads it, and leave a 4 px gap between the title row and the first row, as the website does.

Chapter 16, Photography, illustration and video

A photograph in Oath & Action is a record a reader can check. It shows people doing public work, and its caption says who, what, where and when, then names whoever made it. This chapter covers how we choose, crop, caption and credit photographs, how a frame holds a photograph’s place until we have one, where the few drawn and generated pictures may appear, and how we make and publish video. Two public-domain photographs from federal agencies, the same two chapter 01 shows, teach the photograph rules. Until we commission photographs of our own, a frame that says Placeholder holds each one’s place, and a generated picture appears only with its label and never stands for a real event.

13 rules in four groups; 279,967 bytes against an allocation of 300,000. Each plate below is the guide’s own frame captured at 1440 px from today’s strict build, at true size; open any plate to see it alone.

Group 1: Choosing and framing a photograph

A photograph shows people at public work, in black and white unless its color is part of the record, and its crop and the words around it keep that work in view.

Rule 16.1

Photograph public work while it happens.

Rule 16.2

Show a photograph in black and white, and keep color only where it is part of the record.

Rule 16.3

Crop to keep the act whole, and change nothing inside the frame.

Rule 16.4

Set words beside a photograph, or on a solid panel over a plain part of it.

Group 2: The record behind each photograph

Each photograph runs with a caption a reader can check and alt text for each crop, and our rights ledger, our record of every picture we use, holds its rights and any consent before it runs.

Rule 16.5

Caption who, what, where and when from the record, then credit the maker.

Rule 16.6

Before a photograph runs, record its rights and any consent it needs.

Rule 16.7

Write alt text for what each crop shows.

Group 3: Placeholders and made pictures

A frame that says Placeholder holds the place of a photograph we have yet to make, and a drawn or generated picture appears only where the table after Rule 16.9 allows, with its label.

Rule 16.8

Hold a commissioned photograph’s place with a frame that says Placeholder.

Rule 16.9

Label a generated image beside it, and never let one stand for a real event.

Group 4: Video

A video answers one question. Its soundtrack says what the screen shows, a person checks every caption, its transcript holds every word, and it plays only when a viewer presses Play.
