Six plain steps for the bill tracker, and one job for the grid

You asked for a bill tracker a newcomer can read at once, “like a line with dots or a 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6 step process”, and for the grid to keep one job. This section proposes six plain steps and draws four trackers with them in your four-bill list. It ends with where the lockmark stays.

What we recommend

We recommend six plain steps in place of the nine stages. Option B, numbered steps, fits lists and story cards, and Option D sets the same steps as a timeline on a bill’s own page. The lockmark keeps four fixed places, the masthead, page headers, footers and the sign-off, while every grid that codes something retires.

01

Six steps a newcomer reads at once

Today’s tracker counts nine stages on a 3 by 3 grid, and a reader has to learn the grid’s reading order and five shapes before it tells them anything. Six steps cover the same path. Each has a plain name, and every tracker prints the name with its number, as in “Step 2 of 6: Hearing.”

A chamber is one half of the legislature, the House or the Senate. A bill numbered H. starts in the House, and one numbered S. starts in the Senate, so the words beside each tracker name the real chamber.

The six steps of a state bill, what happens at each, and the stages of today’s tracker each one covers
StepNameWhat happensToday’s stages it covers
1FiledA legislator files the bill, and it goes to a committee.1 Filed, 2 Sent to committee
2HearingThe committee holds a public hearing, where anyone can speak or send written testimony.3 Hearing
3Committee voteThe committee votes on whether to send the bill to the full House or Senate.4 Out of committee
4First chamberThe full House or Senate, wherever the bill was filed, votes on it.5 Passed one chamber
5Second chamberThe other chamber votes, and both agree on one text.6 Passed both chambers, 7 Final text agreed
6GovernorThe governor signs or vetoes the bill, and lawmakers can override a veto. Once all six steps are done, the tracker says “Became law on” with the date, and the bill’s page adds the date it takes effect.8 Signed, 9 In effect

These steps follow a state bill. Chapter 15 sets the steps for other routes, such as a ballot measure or an agency rule, in the same form.

When a bill becomes law

Step 6 is the governor, who signs or vetoes the bill. A bill at step 6 reads “Step 6 of 6: Governor”, which says it is waiting. Once the governor signs, all six steps are done, and the words say “Became law on” with the date. The bill’s page adds the date it takes effect. A veto that stands ends the line at step 6 as Stopped.

Recommendation

Use Option B in lists and on story cards, and Option D on a bill’s own page. They share the six steps and the same numbered marks, so a reader who learns one has learned the other. A number gives a reader words to say, “step 2 of 6”, and it survives a phone screen, gray print and color blindness. A bill’s page has the room the timeline needs for dates, notes and the way to take part.

Option C is the strongest second choice, for a dense list such as a weekly roundup of 20 bills. The dots of Option A are the lightest mark here, though they need their words more than any other option does.

Option A: A line with dots

Six dots sit on one line. The line is solid as far as the bill has come and fine after it, so the line works as a progress bar. Where the page has room, step names sit under the dots; on a phone, the words beside the line name the step.

Our read. It is the calmest of the four. A reader still needs the words to know which dot is which, and a row of nodes on a line comes close to the lockmark’s own drawing.

Our read. We recommend it for lists and story cards, since it reads without a key and six numbered steps fit a phone. Its numbers also set it apart from the lockmark.

Option C: A segmented bar with the stage word

Our read. It is compact, and the stage word reads first. The segments carry no names, so a reader who wants the whole path needs the key, and a slashed segment is small at 12 px.

Accessibility: what was measured

Each state has a name in words. The legend under every list names all of them, and the line beside each tracker names the step a bill is at, any step that never happened and the chance to take part. A screen reader hears every step’s name and state from a list the eye does not see.

Shape alone tells each state apart in grayscale, and color never works alone: the one red mark always comes with its own shape and the words “You can take part now”.

Your answers

Three questions, each with the studio’s recommendation. Nothing in the guide changes until you pick (D2-320). A reply of letters is enough, for example “T1 a, T2 b, T3 a”.

  1. T1

    Do the nine tracker stages become these six steps?

    1. a Six steps, as drawn: Filed, Hearing, Committee vote, First chamber, Second chamber, Governor Recommended
    2. b Keep the nine stages

    Why a. Six steps cover the same path in words a newcomer knows. Each one joins stages a reader seldom needs apart, such as Signed and In effect, whose dates the bill’s page still gives. The hearing keeps a step of its own, since it is the step where the public is invited to speak, and a missing hearing is news a reader should see.

  2. T2

    Which tracker do we use?

    1. a Option A, a line with dots
    2. b Option B in lists and on cards, and Option D on a bill’s own page Recommended
    3. c Option C, the segmented bar
    4. d Option D everywhere

    Why b. Numbers read without a key and hold up on a phone and in gray print. The timeline gives a bill’s page room for dates and the way to take part. Before trackers ship, the six steps and the take-part mark go into the next reader check.

  3. T3

    Does the lockmark keep these four places, with Rules 08.3 to 08.9 retiring?

    1. a Yes: masthead, page header, footer and sign-off, and every coded grid retires Recommended
    2. b Yes, and keep the 16 px legend mark inside charts as a fifth place

    Why a. You asked for one use. A chart footer already holds the lockmark under the footer place, so a separate legend mark adds a fifth spot without a new job.
