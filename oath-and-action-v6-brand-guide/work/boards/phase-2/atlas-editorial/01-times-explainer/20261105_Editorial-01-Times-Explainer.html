What it is

Elections: Who picks them?

Accountability depends on a real chance of losing. In most districts the general election is easy to predict, so the contest that decides the seat is a low-turnout primary. Incumbents also help write the rules for their own races, from district maps to voting procedures. When few officials face a competitive race, the pressure to serve the whole electorate fades.

  • VariableC1

    Seats with little electoral competition

    A higher value means more seats are safe for one party.

    Districts where one party wins by a wide margin, so its candidates face little risk in the general election. The model counts seats won by more than 10 percentage points.

  • VariableC2

    How often the primary decides the winner

    A higher value means more seats are settled in the primary.

    The share of seats where the general election is so predictable that the primary settles who takes office. Turnout in that deciding contest can be far lower than in November.

  • VariableC3

    A higher value means incumbents face a more credible chance of defeat.

    The credible threat that an incumbent will lose the next election. Where seats are competitive, the threat pushes officials to respond to voters before anyone is removed.

  • VariableC4

    Officeholders' control over election rules

    A higher value means incumbents have more say over the rules of their own elections.

    The power of people already in office to set district maps, voting-access rules and local election choices. This is rule-setting authority, and each rule's effect needs its own evidence.

  • VariableC5

    Incumbents defeated or pushed into retirement

    A higher value means more incumbents lose or retire under threat of defeat in an election cycle.

    Officeholders who lose an election or retire under threat of defeat. This consequence is measured separately from the pressure they face while still in office.

Does my vote change who represents me and what they do in office?

  • Registration hurdles and roll errors keep eligible people from votingBrooklyn voters wrongly dropped before a 2016 primary (Brooklyn, New York City, New York, 2016)Well supportedTouches incumbents control election rules

    Eligible people can miss an election because registering is hard or because their record was wrongly removed from the rolls. The burden falls hardest on people who move often.

    The problem, in one place

    Brooklyn voters wrongly dropped before a 2016 primary

    New York City's Board of Elections removed more than 117,000 Brooklyn voters from the rolls before the 2016 presidential primary. In a 2017 consent decree the board admitted it broke state and federal law. It agreed to review removals back to 2013 and restore voters it had wrongly purged.

    Brooklyn, New York City, New York, 2016. Source: WNYC, City Board of Elections Admits It Broke the Law, Accepts Reforms (2017).

    A fix tried somewhere

    Oregon registers voters automatically at the DMV

    Oregon began registering people automatically when they visited the Department of Motor Vehicles, under a 2015 law. More than 230,000 people were added through the program by the 2016 election, and 97,184 of them voted for president.

    Result. Registration grew by more than 230,000, but about 43% of automatic registrants voted in 2016, compared with 79% of people who registered by mail or online.

    Oregon, 2016. Source: Stateline (Pew Charitable Trusts), Oregon's First Test of Automatic Voter Registration Has Mixed Results (2016).

    How it works
    Registration hurdles and flawed maintenance of voter rolls keep some eligible people from voting, and the burden falls unevenly.
    Evidence
    Well supported. The Brennan Center for Justice, a voting-rights research and advocacy organization, counted about 16 million voters removed from rolls nationwide between 2014 and 2016, with removal rates rising in places formerly covered by federal preclearance after Shelby County v. Holder (2013). These are removal rates. The count does not show how many removals were mistakes.
    Proposed response
    States, and Congress for federal elections, would adopt automatic and same-day registration, keep rolls accurate with secure methods, and give voters notice and a chance to correct their record before any removal. Who acts: Congress for federal elections; states.
    Tradeoff
    Rules must protect eligibility checks and data security, and keep noncitizens from being enrolled by mistake.
    How to judge it
    Share of eligible people registered, wrongful removals, and provisional ballots accepted.
  • Long lines make voting cost more in some neighborhoodsMaricopa County cut polling places and lines stretched hours (Maricopa County, Arizona, 2016)Well supportedTouches incumbents control election rules

    In some places voters wait far longer to cast a ballot, often because their polling places get fewer machines and staff. Waiting costs the most for people who cannot leave work.

    The problem, in one place

    Maricopa County cut polling places and lines stretched hours

    Maricopa County, Arizona, opened 60 polling places for its March 2016 presidential primary, down from about 200 four years earlier. One Phoenix voter waited more than five hours, and some people were still in line at midnight. County Recorder Helen Purcell told supervisors she took full responsibility for the cut.

    Maricopa County, Arizona, 2016. Source: Cronkite News (Arizona PBS), Phoenix Mayor Greg Stanton calls for Justice Department investigation of Maricopa County election debacle (2016).

    A fix tried somewhere

    Georgia ties precinct size to hour-long lines

    Georgia's 2021 voting law requires action when voters wait more than an hour at an Election Day precinct. Officials must then cut the number of voters assigned there to no more than 2,000. Election Day lines have been rare in Georgia since the chaotic 2020 primary, the Atlanta Journal-Constitution reported in 2026.

    Result. Average Election Day waits have run about two minutes since 2020, the newspaper reported, though no study isolates this rule's effect and some early-voting sites saw waits of 30 minutes or more in 2026.

    Georgia, 2021 to 2026. Source: Atlanta Journal-Constitution, Georgia voters will see short lines and secure elections, Raffensperger says (2026).

    How it works
    Long lines and limited hours cost more for workers and for voters in minority precincts, where polling places often get fewer resources.
    Evidence
    Well supported. Using smartphone location data from the 2016 election, Chen, Haggag, Pope and Rohla (2019) found that residents of Black neighborhoods waited 29% longer to vote than residents of white neighborhoods; the measurement is strong and covers one election. Pettigrew (2017), working from surveys and observational data, found that minority voters wait longer and traced the gap to how local officials allocate precinct resources. A 2014 Government Accountability Office review of the 2012 election reported that some voters waited over an hour.
    Proposed response
    States and local election offices would set service standards for polling-place access, early voting and mail voting, fund the staff needed to meet them and track the results. Who acts: States; local election offices.
    Tradeoff
    Costs and geography differ from place to place, and any expansion has to keep a secure chain of custody for ballots.
    How to judge it
    Wait times by precinct, and ballot rejection rates.
  • Voters with disabilities and limited English face barriers to voting on their ownHarris County polling places had barriers for disabled voters (Harris County, Texas, 2019)DocumentedTouches incumbents control election rules

    Some voters with disabilities cannot cast a private ballot without help, and some voters who need language assistance do not receive it. Each voting method needs testing with the people who use it.

    The problem, in one place

    Harris County polling places had barriers for disabled voters

    Harris County, Texas, had more than 750 polling places in 2019, the third-largest voting program in the country. The Justice Department alleged that many had barriers such as steep ramps and locked gates. A 2019 settlement required the county to survey nearly two-thirds of its sites and offer effective curbside voting.

    Harris County, Texas, 2019. Source: U.S. Department of Justice, Justice Department Reaches Agreement with Harris County, Texas, to Ensure Polling Place Accessibility (2019).

    A fix tried somewhere

    Ballots in voters' own languages raised participation

    The Voting Rights Act requires counties and towns with sizable language-minority populations to provide ballots and help in those languages. Studying 1,465 jurisdictions in the 2012 election, political scientists Bernard Fraga and Julie Lee Merseth compared places just above and just below the coverage cutoff.

    Result. Coverage under the language provisions brought a significant increase in Latino registration and in Asian American turnout, the 2016 study found.

    U.S. counties and towns covered by the Voting Rights Act's language provisions, 2012 election (study published 2016). Source: Fraga and Merseth, Journal of Race, Ethnicity, and Politics (Cambridge University Press), Examining the Causal Impact of the Voting Rights Act Language Minority Provisions (2016).

    How it works
    Voters with disabilities cannot reliably vote independently, and required language assistance does not always reach the voters who need it.
    Evidence
    Documented. The U.S. Election Assistance Commission's survey series found that the turnout gap between voters with and without disabilities fell from 16.8 points in 2000 to 11.3 points in 2020, with difficulties persisting. In a sample of 178 polling places in 2016, the Government Accountability Office found that 60% had at least one impediment to access.
    Proposed response
    Election administrators would audit every voting method for disability access and provide the language assistance the law requires, testing each with the voters who use it. The Justice Department would enforce these duties. Who acts: Election administrators; DOJ.
    Tradeoff
    Accessible voting technology needs security protections and a backup method.
    How to judge it
    Voters with disabilities who vote independently and successfully, and accessibility complaints.
  • Conviction rules keep millions from votingFlorida tied restored voting rights to paying court debts (Florida, 2018 to 2020)DocumentedTouches incumbents control election rules

    About 4 million Americans could not vote in 2024 because of a felony conviction, under rules that differ by state. Lifetime bans, confusing eligibility and payment conditions keep many excluded after release.

    The problem, in one place

    Florida tied restored voting rights to paying court debts

    Florida voters restored voting rights to most people with felony convictions in 2018. The next year lawmakers required them to settle all fines and restitution first. In 2020 a federal appeals court upheld that rule 6-4, leaving people who cannot pay their court debts unable to vote.

    Florida, 2018 to 2020. Source: Associated Press (via Boston Globe), Florida felons can't vote until they pay fines, fees, appeals court rules (2020).

    A fix tried somewhere

    Minnesota restored the vote on release from prison

    Minnesota's 2023 law restores voting rights to people with felony convictions once they leave prison, instead of after probation ends. An estimated 55,000 people gained the right to vote. In August 2024 the Minnesota Supreme Court rejected a challenge to the law.

    Result. About 55,000 people regained eligibility; AP reported no count of how many have since registered or voted.

    Minnesota, 2023 to 2024. Source: Associated Press, Minnesota Supreme Court upholds law restoring right to vote to people with felony convictions (2024).

    How it works
    Lifetime or confusing voting exclusions after a criminal conviction keep people from rejoining political life.
    Evidence
    Documented. The Sentencing Project, an advocacy organization, estimated that about 4.0 million people were barred from voting by a felony conviction in 2024, including 4.5% of Black adults compared with 1.3% of other adults. The estimate comes from a model built on correctional data.
    Proposed response
    States, and Congress where it has authority, would restore voting rights on release from prison, notify eligible people automatically and remove payment conditions that make voting depend on wealth. Who acts: States; Congress where authorized.
    Tradeoff
    Eligibility law varies by state, and constitutional litigation may be needed.
    How to judge it
    Registrations by people whose rights were restored, and eligibility errors.
  • DC and territorial residents have no vote in CongressCongress overturned a DC law without a DC vote (Washington, DC, 2023)DocumentedTouches safe seats, seats settled in the primary, risk of losing office, incumbents control election rules and incumbents defeated

    About 700,000 people in Washington, DC, along with residents of the U.S. territories, have no voting member of the House or Senate. Changing that takes legislation or a constitutional amendment, and territorial residents' own choices about political status.

    The problem, in one place

    Congress overturned a DC law without a DC vote

    In March 2023, the House voted 250-173 to overturn the District of Columbia's rewrite of its criminal code, and the Senate and President Biden followed. Congress had not nullified a DC law this way in more than three decades. DC's only member of Congress, Delegate Eleanor Holmes Norton, holds a nonvoting seat.

    Washington, DC, 2023. Source: Associated Press, Biden upends politics, precedent in pivot on DC crime law (2023).

    A fix tried somewhere

    A 1961 amendment gave DC a vote for president

    The Twenty-third Amendment, ratified on March 29, 1961, gave District of Columbia residents presidential electors, capped at the number held by the least populous state. DC residents first voted for president in 1964. The amendment left them without a voting member of Congress.

    Result. DC has taken part in every presidential election since 1964, while its seat in Congress remains nonvoting.

    Washington, DC, 1961. Source: National Archives (Reagan Library blog), Constitutional Amendments Series: Amendment XXIII, Extending the Vote to the District of Columbia (2022).

    How it works
    Residents of Washington, DC and the U.S. territories have no voting representation in Congress.
    Evidence
    Documented. The Census Bureau puts the District of Columbia's population at about 700,000, and the District has no voting member of the House or Senate.
    Proposed response
    Congress would pursue representation for DC and a political status for each territory chosen by its residents, through lawful legislation and, where needed, a constitutional amendment. Who acts: Congress; affected residents.
    Tradeoff
    Residents' consent, the constitutional structure and territorial self-determination all have to be respected.
    How to judge it
    Residents with full voting representation in Congress.
  • Map-drawers lock in a party's seatsBoth parties drew congressional maps to win seats (North Carolina and Maryland, 2011 to 2019)Well supportedTouches safe seats, risk of losing office, incumbents control election rules and incumbents defeated

    Officials who draw district lines can arrange them so their party keeps its seats even as voters change their minds. Federal courts do not hear these claims, so state rules and commissions decide how far it goes.

    The problem, in one place

    Both parties drew congressional maps to win seats

    In North Carolina, Republican legislator David Lewis proposed a 2016 congressional map electing 10 Republicans and 3 Democrats, and Republicans won 10 of 13 seats. Maryland Democrats in 2011 moved about 360,000 voters out of one district to flip it. In Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), the Supreme Court ruled federal courts cannot hear such claims.

    North Carolina and Maryland, 2011 to 2019. Source: Supreme Court of the United States, Rucho v. Common Cause (2019).

    A fix tried somewhere

    Arizona voters handed map-drawing to an independent commission

    Arizona voters adopted Proposition 106 in 2000, moving congressional map-drawing from the legislature to an independent commission. No more than two of its five members may belong to the same party, and the chair must come from outside those parties. The legislature sued, and in 2015 the Supreme Court upheld the commission 5-4.

    Result. The ruling let voter-created commissions keep drawing congressional maps; the Court cited studies finding that commissions draw maps on time and create more competitive districts.

    Arizona, 2000 to 2015. Source: Legal Information Institute, Cornell Law School (Supreme Court opinion), Arizona State Legislature v. Arizona Independent Redistricting Commission (2015).

    How it works
    When the party in power draws district maps, it can entrench itself despite shifts in voters' preferences.
    Evidence
    Well supported. In Rucho v. Common Cause (2019), the Supreme Court ruled that federal courts cannot hear partisan gerrymandering claims; state law and other federal voting protections still apply. Warshaw, McGhee and Migurski (2022) found that maps drawn by commissions and courts in the 2021 to 2022 cycle showed less partisan bias than maps drawn by legislatures, using descriptive measures from one cycle. The Congressional Research Service has documented the mid-decade redraws of 2025 and 2026.
    Proposed response
    States, and Congress for House elections, would give map-drawing to independent commissions, publish draft maps, set explicit neutral criteria and allow enforceable review in state courts. Who acts: States; Congress for House rules.
    Tradeoff
    Geography limits how many districts can be competitive, and neutral criteria can conflict with protecting minority voters' ability to elect candidates.
    How to judge it
    Partisan bias measured across a range of possible vote outcomes, and districts where minority voters can elect their preferred candidates.

Reform R-01 · Rules · All-party primaries

All-party primaries with ranked general elections

Every voter, whatever their party, could vote in one first-round election, and two or more candidates would advance to a November ballot that all voters use. In seats that one party usually wins, the choice now made in a low-turnout party primary would move to the full electorate. In Massachusetts, unenrolled voters already pick one party's ballot. Under Question 3, every voter would see all candidates on one primary ballot from 2028. The Atlas takes no position on Question 3 or Ballot Measure 2.

Status

As of October 15, 2026, two measures on the November 3, 2026 ballot would change how primaries work. Massachusetts voters decide Question 3, which would replace party primaries for state and congressional offices with one primary listing every candidate, the top two advancing to November; if approved, it takes effect January 1, 2028. Evidence

The problem is better established than the remedy.

Evidence of the problem

Plausible: some evidence

Cook Political Report rated 398 of 435 House seats safe for 2026, as reported by Unite America, an advocacy organization that funds primary-reform campaigns. Unite America's own count puts 383 seats (88%) as effectively decided in the 2026 primaries, which drew about 22.7 million voters, 9.4% of eligible voters. Both counts rest on ratings, which are forecasts, and on the group's definition of a safe seat.

Evidence for the remedy

Contested: evidence disputed

More contested general elections and more candidates entering races are plausible results. Whether legislators become more moderate is contested: a 2014 study of state legislators by McGhee and colleagues found that more open primaries had little consistent effect on ideology, and experimental work finds that primary effects depend on conditions. Main limitation: Alaska's top-four system has a short record, so there is no long-run U.S. test of a top-four primary paired with a ranked general election.

Tradeoffs

Parties lose control over who carries their label, and some voters lose a party ballot line they value. Ranked tabulation takes longer to count. When two candidates from the same party advance, voters from the other party have no nominee of their own in November.

How it could fail

Party organizations in both major parties and incumbents secure in their primaries are expected to resist. Parties can coordinate so that only one of their candidates competes; in Alaska in 2024 one candidate withdrew after the top-four primary, with party consultants citing the risk of splitting the vote. Voters or legislatures can reverse the rule, and nineteen states ban ranked-choice voting in some or all elections. The reform can also fall short if turnout stays low in the deciding round, or if voters misread the ballot and trust in results drops.

What it costs

Implementation is a small one-time cost for state and county election offices. In Massachusetts, Question 3 would take effect January 1, 2028, leaving time to prepare. Party leaders and incumbents who are secure in their primaries lose influence, first as a threat and in practice at the next open seat.

The law

States control primary rules within limits the Supreme Court has set. In Jones (2000) the Court barred blanket primaries that force parties to accept nominees chosen by all voters. In Washington State Grange (2008) it upheld a top-two primary that does not name party nominees. A ranked general election also requires a state with no ban on ranked ballots.

Carrying it out

Supporters first draft a measure and qualify it for the ballot, or a legislature passes a bill. A campaign follows. Before the first election under the new rules, election offices teach voters how the ballot works. After two election cycles, results can be judged by turnout in the round that decides each seat and by the number of contested general elections.

How to judge results

  • Share of seats with a competitive general election
  • Turnout in the round that decides each seat
  • Share of incumbents facing a credible general-election challenger
  • Voter understanding of the ballot, measured in exit surveys
  • Legislators' voting ideology, reported as exploratory because the best existing tests found no effect

Other options

Open partisan primaries let voters outside a party choose that party's ballot. Fusion voting (R-47) is another option, and proportional House districts (R-03) change the general election and leave the primary as it is. Two options are set aside as rejected. Blanket primaries are unconstitutional under Jones (2000). Turnout drives in closed-primary states raise participation and leave the rules on who may vote in a primary as they are.

How to read the atlas

Every line says what happens next, and how sure we are.

The atlas uses one small language on every page. Five ideas cover it. “A quantity or condition in the model that can rise or fall. Its definition states what a higher or lower value means.”

  1. 1

    The tip is the effect.

    An arrow runs from a cause to what it changes. The tag on the line carries the sign, and the word beside it says what the sign means: raises or lowers.

    A plus sign means the two variables move the same way. It does not mean the change is good.

    L08: Safe seats make the primary decisive. From C1 Safe seats to C2 Seats settled in the primary. Sign +, it raises it. Evidence documented. Delay under 2 years.

    L25: Decisive primaries reduce general-election pressure. From C2 Seats settled in the primary to C3 Risk of losing office. Sign −, it lowers it. Evidence well supported. Delay under 2 years.

    7 lines of authority carry no sign. They end in an open diamond and name who decides.

    LE02: Congress decides public investment levels. From D1 Congress's capacity to EC2 Public investment. A line of authority with no sign: one side decides. Evidence documented. Delay 2 to 10 years.

  2. 2

    Texture is the evidence.

    The more broken the line, the weaker the evidence behind it. Every connection carries one of five grades from Atlas V7.

    • Documented: on the public record. 32 connections.

    • Well supported: strong research evidence. 37 connections.

    • Plausible: some evidence. 35 connections.

    • Contested: evidence disputed. 7 connections.

    • Hypothesis: untested. 7 connections.

  3. 3

    Ticks count the wait.

    The time between a change and its effect. Small ticks just after the tail of an arrow show how long: one, two or three.

    • Under 2 years. 38 connections.

    • 2 to 10 years. 45 connections.

    • Over 10 years. 35 connections.

  4. 4

    Loops have a character.

    A chain of causes that returns to its starting variable. Reinforcing loops amplify change and balancing loops counteract it; either kind can produce good or harmful results. Count the minus signs around a loop: an even number means it feeds itself, an odd number means it pushes back.

    18 loops feed themselves and 5 push back. “Improvement or decline can reinforce itself.”

    More quality of public services, less risk of losing office, fewer incumbents defeated, and back to less quality of public services.

Easy to mix up

Three readings the atlas warns against.

  • A district where the incumbent could lose but never does: is that pressure, sanction, both or neither?

    Pressure without sanction. Risk of losing office can be present while incumbents defeated stays at zero; the accountability loop needs both.

  • A line labelled auth, with no plus or minus sign, connects Congress to public investment. What does it mean?

    Authority: Congress has the power to decide; the line does not say which way it will.

  • Why does the atlas not give each reform a single score?

    The four outcome families use different units, some reforms help one and cost another, and the evidence differs in quality.
