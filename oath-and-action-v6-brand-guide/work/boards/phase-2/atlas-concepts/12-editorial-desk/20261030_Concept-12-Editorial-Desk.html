OverviewArea CLoop R10

Causal claim · documented and well supported

In a safe seat, officials answer to the primary.

With little threat in November, they may then write election rules that keep the seat safe. That loop is one of 23 in this map of who officials answer to.

Every loop, drawn as its own route

Of the 23 loops, 18 feed themselves, and 22 have a reform aimed at them.

A loop is a chain of causes that returns to where it started. The other 5 push back. The model warns that a loop is no verdict: Improvement or decline can reinforce itself. Each small map shows the loop’s route across the system, with the same marks as the drawing above.

Two causal loops · B4 well supported, R10 plausible at its weakest step

At electoral pressure, a loop that pushes back meets one that feeds itself.

Where seats are competitive, poor service raises an incumbent’s risk of losing, defeats follow, and the officials who remain do better. Safe seats drain that pressure, and the officials they protect may write the rules that keep them safe.

Loop R10 · Reinforcing, feeds itself · plausible at its weakest step

Safe seats shift accountability to small primary electorates

Three of its four steps are documented or well supported. The weakest, L53, is graded plausible, so the loop holds only as firmly as that step. One turn takes 2 to 10 years.

Read it aloud

More safe seats, more primary decisiveness, less electoral pressure, more incumbent control of election rules, and back to more safe seats.

In a safe seat, the primary decides who wins, and few people vote in it. With little threat in November, officeholders answer to that smaller electorate and face less pressure from the wider public. Officials who cannot easily be removed can then write election rules that keep seats safe.

Keep in mind

Seat safety also comes from where voters live, and the claim that primary reform makes legislators more moderate is contested, so the loop concerns accountability more than ideology.

Now: running up, measured by the share of House seats settled in primaries, 2012 to 2024.

The model reads it this way

Seats with little electoral competition raise how often the primary decides the winner, which lowers the risk of losing office; lower risk raises officeholders' control over election rules, which raises the number of seats with little electoral competition.

L08: Safe seats make the primary decisive and L42: Incumbent-drawn maps create safe seats are documented, and L25: Decisive primaries reduce general-election pressure is well supported. The weakest step is L53: Officials safe from defeat write rules for themselves, graded plausible: its evidence is the 2025 mid-decade redistricting in states with one-party control and legal analysis. Whether primaries make legislators more extreme is a separate, contested question, and the strongest tests found no effect.
