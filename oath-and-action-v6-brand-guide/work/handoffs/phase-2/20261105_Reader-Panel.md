# Reader Panel: Five Versions of the Atlas

Simulated usability and comprehension panel, November 5, 2026. The only source was the screenshots in each version's `captures/` folder (desktop at 1440 and phone at 390, cut into screen-sized pieces). No HTML, script, brief, note or report was opened. For version D, the panel used the standard capture names and set aside the extra color and state captures.

**The readers (simulated personas; every quote in this report is simulated).** Dana, a newsletter subscriber in her 40s who skims on her phone. Luis, a community college student assigned to explain how a law gets shaped. Tasha, a local organizer who wants to know where to push. Walt, a retired engineer who distrusts anything that smells partisan. Priya, a busy parent who wants to know why rent and bills keep rising.

**How answers were graded.** Each reader gave seven graded answers per version: (1) the ten-second read of the first overview screen, (2) the parts and arrows of the full overview, (3) whether R10 closes and whether it feeds itself, (4) R10's least certain step and where the reform acts, (5) R-01's problem, change and stance, (6) one Elections problem and one fix, (7) whether the phone overview could be followed. That makes 35 answers per version. Ratings are not graded. "Right" means the answer matches what the screenshots show. For R-01's stance, a right answer saw both "The Atlas takes no position on Question 3 or Ballot Measure 2" and the editor's disclosure, and read them correctly. Figures quoted from the pages (seat counts, turnout shares, vote margins) appear as the screenshots show them; the panel did not check them against their sources.

The misses cluster in three places across all versions. The ten-second test produced 13 partly right answers out of 25: in D the map sits below the first screen, and in E the first screen shows five question topics instead of the system's parts. The phone overview produced 10 partly right answers and the panel's only wrong one. The R-01 stance question produced 7 partly right answers, at least one in every version: where the disclosure sits near the top (A, C, D), Priya read it as the Atlas backing the reform; where it sits at the bottom of the reform page (B, E), Dana and Priya never reached it. The loop pages were the best understood part of every version: 47 of 50 R10 answers were right.

## 2. Version by version

### A, Times Explainer

White and newspaper-like. The overview reads as an article: a numbered spine from Outside forces through parts 1 to 7, with a sentence between each pair ("5 connections run from Wealth to Voice, such as...").

**Biggest confusions**

1. The everyday questions come last. On desktop, "16 everyday questions lead into the map" is on the seventh of eight screens; on the phone it is about fifteen screens down. Priya: "I scrolled past seven parts before anything said rent. The only rent near the top was an example in the how-to-read box."
2. The whole-system drawing is cut off on the phone, mid-word at "Voi", behind a "View the drawing full size" button. Tasha: "The one picture of the whole system stops at Voi, and I had to leave the page to see the rest."
3. Codes and shorthand. Luis: "L01, LE12, LT08, T11: I can't tell what the letters mean. And '+S (small positive)' and a label called 'Parameter' slowed me down." Walt tripped on naming: "A rule that cuts household security runs back to Wealth, because Households' financial security lives in Wealth and Your household is a separate part. Two things called household in two parts." Priya also took R10's red marks for the bad step: "I thought red meant the problem."

**Liked most**

1. Every number says what it counts. Luis: "'4 connections run from Elections to Checks, such as Electoral pressure restrains unilateral presidential action.' Count plus example. I could put that in my paper."
2. The reform page puts the doubt up front. Walt: "'The problem is better established than the remedy.' And the disclosure sits beside Status at the top, where I'd look for it." He had flagged the main headline, "Wealth at the top can shape the rules that add to it," as "a thesis before I've seen the evidence," and said the reform page won him back.
3. Elections problems grouped under plain questions. Tasha: "'Who gets a meeting with my representative, and what does it cost them?' That's how my members talk."

### B, Economist Register

Warm gray, data-desk style. A horizontal circuit row, then a register table with one row per part (counts, evidence bars, main loop); the Elections row opens to show story, chain and fix.

**Biggest confusions**

1. Numbers without nouns. The first screen shows "5 in" and "4 in" above the boxes; below the circuit, "7 in 4 out" floats under Wealth. Dana: "I thought the big numbers were scores for how strong each part is." Luis: "Is '7 in' seven into my household or seven into Wealth? 'In means into that part' doesn't say which part."
2. "The Prussian tab marks where R-01 acts," on the overview and the R10 page. Dana: "What's a Prussian tab?" Priya: "I assumed it was a typo."
3. The stance and the disclosure are split. "The Atlas takes no position" sits under the reform headline; the disclosure sits at the very bottom, inside Status. Dana and Priya stopped before it. Walt found it: "The no-position line is at the top and the conflict is at the bottom. Put them side by side." Priya was also thrown by the household row: "'Your household: 0 loops. No loop runs through this part.' So my rent doesn't loop? Then why does it keep going up?"

**Liked most**

1. Evidence first. Walt: "The five evidence grades are on the first screen, and the register says 'The evidence is thinnest at Policy, where the circuit closes.' Someone is grading their own homework honestly."
2. Problem, loop and fix in one row. Tasha: "Open Elections and you get 'Whether voters can fire them,' the safe-seat chain marked 'R-01 acts here,' and the fix in the blue box. One glance."
3. Real-world anchors. Luis on R10's "A real case": "Alaska's top-four survived a repeal by 743 votes out of 321,203. That's the detail a teacher wants." Tasha on "Four routes, from a state ballot to Congress": "Feasibility for each route, with a last-checked date." Dana also found B's phone circuit, drawn top to bottom, easy to follow.

### C, Reference Sheets

Technical-sheet style: numbered sheets 00 to 05, plates, a key plan, five tall cards, crimson targets marking R-01.

**Biggest confusions**

1. The wiring under the cards. Between the five cards and Your household and Technology runs a tangle of crossing lines with floating numbers and "THE RETURN LINE" printed in the middle. Tasha: "It's a subway map without colors. I can't tell which 2 goes to Checks and which goes to Policy." Dana: "I gave up on the lines and read the cards."
2. Manual vocabulary: "Sheet 02," "Plate 01.2," "Key plan," and on Elections, "Kind: Stock (an amount built up over time)," "Condition (auxiliary)," "small positive (V7: +S entry; -S extremity)." Priya: "Plates and sheets. It feels like homework." Luis: "Why is risk of losing office 'auxiliary'?"
3. Two counts for one claim on the reform page. The problem box gives 383 seats, 88 percent and 9.4 percent turnout for 2026; the issue card beside it gives 380 seats, 87 percent and 7 percent for 2024. Walt: "Pick a year or say why you show both." Priya, meanwhile, read the disclosure near the top as a stance: "The editor works for Yes on 3, so this page is for it."

**Liked most**

1. The R10 sheet teaches the rule. Luis: "'Start at C1, top left, and follow the arrows clockwise... An even number of lowering steps makes a loop reinforcing.' Now I can explain why it feeds itself." The "Loop R10 at a glance" table (weakest step L53, plausible) helped every reader.
2. Questions that show their route. Priya: "'Why did my rent go up? Runs through Wealth, Your household and Technology, Loop R8.' That's on the third screen and tells me where to look."
3. The phone. Dana: "Each card ends with '4 connections pass on to Elections' and the next card starts. The easiest phone version to follow."

### D, Night Edition

Dark background, light type, red highlights. A strong headline over a clean five-card map with the economy beneath, framed by two questions: "Who does our country work for?" and "Who do they answer to?"

**Biggest confusions**

1. No map on the first screen. It holds a three-line headline, two question cards and a paragraph; the map begins at the bottom edge. Asked for the main parts, Dana, Tasha and Priya named "Democracy" and "The Economy," the two cards. Walt: "I count two parts and a lot of adjectives."
2. The phone drops the map. It becomes a stack of cards with lists such as "Passes on to Voice 5, Your household 4," while the intro still says "read from left to right" and "Along the bottom, the return line." Dana: "Left to right on a phone? What bottom?" Luis: "I couldn't find the circle anywhere." All five readers could only partly follow it.
3. The headline reads as a verdict: "Policy that favors insiders adds to the wealth that funds political spending." Walt: "That's a conclusion dressed as a headline. And gray text on black at this size is hard on my eyes." On R10, Priya was pulled off course by extra labels: "R-01 is red, but there's also 'R-03 aims here' and 'R-02 aims here.' Which reform are we talking about?"

**Liked most**

1. The map itself. Luis: "Five cards, a plain question on each, arrows with counts, one return line. And 'Not drawn, to keep the map calm: 15 smaller connections' tells me what's missing." Walt called that "the most honest sentence on the page."
2. The kitchen-table opening. Priya: "'Rent climbs faster than pay.' Yes. And 'Start from your own bill' is on the third screen." Dana: "Looks like a magazine at night. I'd open it."
3. The reform page. Tasha: "'Who could adopt it': voters in initiative states, medium; legislatures, low; Congress, very low. That's my planning sheet." Walt: "'Confidence: medium (participation); low (moderation).' Good."

### E, Architecture

White and icon-rich. The system is a building: Technology and Your household at the base, then Wealth up to Policy on top, outside forces in a column beside it, a return line down the left.

**Biggest confusions**

1. The first screen shows question topics and leaves the system for later. Under "Who do they answer to?" come five question cards (Cost of living, Health and care and so on); the building starts on the second screen. Every reader was only partly right on the main parts. Tasha: "Where's the power map? I see five topics." Luis on the intro: "'Start from an entry document, an ordinary paper a reader already handles... used as a starting point into the map.' I read it twice."
2. On the phone the building reads against its own arrows. Policy comes first, then Checks, Elections, Voice and Wealth, with arrows labeled "pass up." Dana gave the panel's one wrong answer: "Policy hands things down to Checks, then to Elections, then to Voice." The how-to-read panel arrives after the building.
3. The metaphor raises its own questions. Priya: "Why is my household under the ground line? And what's 'on Newsprint'?" Walt: "Putting Wealth on the ground floor of politics is itself a claim." The R-01 disclosure is at the bottom of the reform page, after Status; Dana and Priya missed it there. It also appears beside the reform cards on R10, which neither read closely.

**Liked most**

1. Questions first. Priya: "'Why did my rent go up?' was the first thing I saw. I'd click that before anything."
2. Caveats where the claim is. Walt on R10: "'No study measures this link directly.' 'Residential sorting also produces safe seats without any map drawing.' That's how you write for someone like me." "The evidence, graded twice" on the reform page got the same reaction.
3. Plain explanations and failure modes. Tasha: "'How it could fail: party organizations in both major parties... are expected to resist.' Nobody else told me who pushes back." Luis on the Elections reforms: "'This is called the spoiler problem.' Finally a definition." The Elections floor plan, five rooms in one row, was the clearest Elections drawing in the set.

## 4. Overall ranking

1. **A, Times Explainer.** Effectively tied with C on comprehension (28 right to 29), no wrong answers, and the clearest rating after E. Its overview explains every number in a sentence, so no reader needed the legend. Its problems are fixable: move the questions up and make the phone drawing fit.
2. **C, Reference Sheets.** The best loop page and the easiest phone, but its main overview drawing was the hardest picture any reader faced, and its vocabulary (sheets, plates, stock, auxiliary) costs general readers.
3. **E, Architecture.** The best-liked and most trusted, with the strongest inner pages (R10 caveats, reform failure modes, the Elections floor plan). It ranks third because its first screen hides the parts and its phone order runs backward, and those are the moments when first-time readers decide whether to stay.
4. **B, Economist Register.** Trusted for its evidence grading and its problem, loop and fix row, but dense with unlabeled numbers, it carries "Prussian tab," and it separates the stance from the disclosure.
5. **D, Night Edition.** The most striking to look at, with a clean desktop map, but the lowest comprehension, a headline the skeptic read as advocacy, a phone overview without the map, and low-contrast text.

The change with the largest payoff in every version is the first screen: put the parts and the circuit on it, named in words a reader can repeat.

## 5. These readers are simulated

One AI model wrote and voiced all five readers. Their answers, quotes, ratings and favorites are predictions of how such people might react to the screenshots. The same model graded the answers, so the right, partly right and wrong counts come from one judge marking its own work. With five personas, a gap of one to three answers between versions is noise: A and C are effectively tied, and D and E are close.

What the panel checked:
- Every desktop and phone capture of the six pages in all five versions, cut into screen-sized pieces; the long Elections and loop catalog pages were sampled at their key sections.
- The first desktop screen of each overview, on its own, for the ten-second test.
- Each answer against the text and drawings visible in the screenshots.

What the panel could not check:
- Interaction: Play buttons, tap-to-read panels, "Select a count," reform pickers and collapsed sections are frozen in the captures.
- Reading time, scroll fatigue, eyesight, distraction and real devices.
- How real readers with these backgrounds would answer.
- Whether the figures on the pages match their sources.

Treat this report as a set of hypotheses. Test the first-screen, phone and disclosure findings first, with five to eight real first-time readers on the same tasks, before choosing a version.
