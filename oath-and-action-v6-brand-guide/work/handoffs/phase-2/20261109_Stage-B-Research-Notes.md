## 1. Meaning sheet for the drawings board (D2-502)

### Read this first: four things that touch every drawing

1. **Two drawings already draw a legislative chamber.** R-03 Proportional Elections draws a hemicycle (a half-ring of seats) as dots with a red block at the top center. R-46 Expand the House draws the chamber from above, with a rostrum and red seats added at the right end. DR-146's House-chamber option for CL-025 will sit beside both. Each chamber drawing needs a feature the other two lack, and the shape-likeness check should compare all three. One way to separate them: R-03 is about how seats are shared, R-46 is about more seats, and Congress is the body itself, so Congress could show two chambers (one large, one small, for the House and the Senate) or show the chamber as the gallery sees it, in elevation, where R-03 and R-46 are seen from above.
2. **Red on one side of a chamber reads as party.** A red block of seats at the left or right of a hemicycle reads as one party's seats (Rule 01.1, and DR-114's question on reading Pillar Red for party). DR-146 puts Congress's red on the rostrum, at the center. Keep any new chamber drawing's red on the center axis or off the seats. R-46 already puts red seats on the right end; that drawing is settled, so I raise it only as a question (question 4 at the end).
3. **Use one sign for Congress across the four dome drawings.** CL-025, R-13 and R-42 all mean Congress, and CL-014 means a legislature or another rule-setting body. If each replaces its dome with a different picture, a reader learns four signs for one institution. I recommend one Congress sign, drawn once and reused smaller in R-13 and R-42, and the same chamber without the Congress-only feature in CL-014.
4. **Signs already taken in the set.** These are in use and would make a new drawing read as another subject:
   - padlock: R-12 Filibuster Reform (red padlock on a desk)
   - two speech bubbles: R-12, CL-008 Political Hostility
   - person, arrow and envelope: CL-061 Household Income
   - official at a desk with a red tie: CL-026 Executive Power
   - two people facing across a table with a red paper: CL-016 Lobbying
   - speaker at a lectern with listeners: CL-005 Public Meeting, CL-002 Representation
   - gavel: R-11 Election Enforcement; scales: CL-027 Court Alignment, R-16 Court Terms & Ethics
   - open book: CL-019 Explain (a pillar icon), CL-068 Education, and R-13 itself
   - coin stacks: CL-049, CL-062, R-07, R-26, R-28; dollar coin: CL-015, CL-036; piggy bank: R-29
   - gabled hall with a red door, no columns: CL-031 Local Government

Rule 01.6 keeps a capitol dome, columns and a flag out of icons. Rule 01.7 keeps party symbols, candidate photographs, campaign gear and protest slogans out of brand graphics. Both bind every option below.

### CL-025 Congress

- **V7 name and ID.** Not in V7 as its own element. The studio's icon map assigns CL-025 to **D1, "Congress's ability to write and check policy"** (V7's short name "Congressional capacity"; the concept boards say "Congress's capacity"; V7 sheet 05; a stock; loops R2 and R13; domain D04).
- **V7's meaning.** D1 is "the staff and independent expertise Congress needs to investigate problems, draft laws and oversee how agencies carry them out." The icon's own word is "Congress", the institution itself: the two-chamber national legislature, the House and the Senate.
- **What a reader must recognize.** A lawmaking body of many elected members who sit together, debate and vote. If the drawing can carry one more idea, that the body has two chambers, which separates Congress from a state legislature, a city council and a foreign parliament.
- **What would mislead.**
  - A dome, columns or a flag behind the rostrum (Rule 01.6). The House chamber has a large flag behind the Speaker's rostrum, so a gallery view must leave it out.
  - Seats split into a red side and a black side (party control, Rule 01.1).
  - An eagle, a seal or the mace, which few readers know and which read as official insignia.
  - A gavel alone, which reads as a court (R-11, CL-027).
  - A lectern facing a crowd, which reads as a campaign speech (CL-002, CL-005).
  - A plan view of dots or seats, too close to R-03 and R-46 (note 1).

### CL-014 Entrenched Power

- **V7 name and ID.** **D7, "Entrenched control of rule-setting institutions"** (V7's short name "Entrenched majority control"; V7 sheet 03; a stock; loops R4a and R12; domains D02 and D05).
- **V7's meaning.** "Lasting control of a legislature or another body that sets election rules or confirms judges", which safe seats help a majority keep and use again. V7's why: "A majority that cannot lose can keep redrawing the rules in its own favor."
- **What a reader must recognize.** A rule-setting body, most often a legislature, and one majority closed in so the next election cannot easily move it. Today's fence carries the second idea well; the dome carried the first and must go. V7 counts the Senate that confirms judges as such a body, so the chamber should stay generic and not say "state" or "Congress" alone.
- **What would mislead.**
  - A padlock (R-12 Filibuster Reform, and a likely utility glyph for "locked").
  - A crown or throne, which reads as monarchy and echoes the "No Kings" protest slogan of 2025 (Rule 01.7).
  - A red block of seats inside the fence. It would read as one party's majority. The icon is general and names no party; any party count belongs in copy with its source (DR-93).
  - A castle or fortress, which reads as medieval or military.
  - A single chair inside a boundary, which is CL-009 Safe Seats.

### R-13 Congressional Capacity

- **V7 name and ID.** **R-13, "Rebuild Congress's own staff and experts"** (V7 sheets 04 and 05; routes: federal statute, and chamber rule or confirmation; it acts on links L18 and L19 and raises D1).
- **V7's meaning.** "Congress would hire and keep more of its own analysts, on committees and at support agencies such as the Congressional Research Service, the Congressional Budget Office and the Government Accountability Office," so members depend less on lobbyists to draft and assess bills.
- **What a reader must recognize.** Congress (the same sign as CL-025, smaller), its own staff (people standing with or inside it), and their expert work (reports or analysis). If there is room, "more": the staff row grows, or one figure is being added.
- **What would mislead.**
  - Figures with briefcases or suits facing the building from outside, which read as lobbyists (CL-016). The reform exists to reduce reliance on lobbyists.
  - The open book is the sign of CL-019 Explain, a pillar icon, and of CL-068 Education. Today's red page edge makes it close to Explain. An option that may change more (D2-495) could swap the book for a stacked report or a page with a chart line, which reads as analysis.
  - A dome or columns (Rule 01.6).

### R-42 Congressional Tariff Votes

- **V7 name and ID.** **R-42, "Congress votes on lasting tariffs"** (V7 sheet 12; routes: federal statute, and court doctrine; loop R13; links LE18 and LE19).
- **V7's meaning.** "Tariffs, which are taxes on imports, could stay in place beyond a short period only if Congress voted to approve them."
- **What a reader must recognize.** Congress (the shared sign), a vote by its members, and imported goods with a tax on them. "Beyond a short period" can be left to the label.
- **What would mislead.**
  - The ballot box. It is the sign of a public election (CL-001 Voting, CL-015 Political Money), so a reader may think voters decide tariffs. Members cast recorded votes. Within DR-135 the ballot box stays; an option that may change more could use a yes and no tally, raised hands, or a vote board on the chamber.
  - The right-hand part. The inventory calls it "a small rising bar chart" and asks the redraw to keep it, but at 256 px it reads as a ribbed shipping container under an arrow. If the designers keep it as a chart, it should read as a chart; if an option turns it into goods, use the crate already drawn in CL-064 Trade Costs and CL-036 Cross-Border Capital so the set keeps one sign for imports.
  - A national flag on a crate or container (Rule 01.6), which would also single out a country.
  - A dollar sign on the goods, which collides with CL-015 and CL-036.

### CL-059 Credit Allocation

- **V7 name and ID.** **EC5, "Where newly created credit goes"** (V7 has no shorter name for it, since it sits on no loop; the concept boards say "Where credit goes"; V7 sheet 12; a flow).
- **V7's meaning.** "Banks create deposits when they lend, so lending directs new purchasing power toward homes, businesses and other uses." V7's why: lending decides whether new money flows into homes and businesses or into bidding up assets people already own.
- **What a reader must recognize.** A bank or lender at the source, and new money splitting toward different uses, at least homes and businesses. The red branching path does the second job now and stays (DR-135). The pediment and columns did the first job, so the replacement must still say "bank".
- **What would mislead.**
  - Columns or a pediment (Rule 01.6), and a Federal Reserve seal or eagle.
  - A dollar coin, coin stacks or a piggy bank. Those signs belong to CL-015, CL-049, CL-062 and R-29, and a piggy bank means saving, the opposite of lending.
  - A vault door alone, which reads as storing money; if used, the red branching path below it has to carry the idea of money going out.
  - A gabled hall with a door, which is CL-031 Local Government.
- **Note for the designers.** No column-free bank sign is common. A building with a vault-door circle on its face, a teller counter, or a loan paper stamped beside a small building are candidates, each to be checked against the list in note 4.

### R-48 Agency Independence

- **V7 name and ID.** **R-48, "Agency independence after the Slaughter ruling"** (V7 sheets 05 and 06; routes: federal statute, and court doctrine; loop R18; links L14 and L15).
- **V7's meaning.** "After the Supreme Court's June 2026 decision in Trump v. Slaughter, Congress would protect independent agencies through other tools: public reasons and records for removals, protected budgets, agency structures the ruling leaves intact, and a written exception for the Federal Reserve."
- **What a reader must recognize.** An independent agency, a power that can remove its officials, and a protective separation between the two. V7's success measures speak of multi-member agencies and their quorum, so a commission (several officials seated at one long table) is a column-free way to say "agency". The red brace already says "protected separation" and stays (DR-135); R-17 Civil Service uses the same brace for protections, which helps the family.
- **What would mislead.**
  - Columns (Rule 01.6), and the White House, which has columns and names one building.
  - A presidential seal or any likeness of a real president (Rule 01.7).
  - The lectern on the right. It reads as a speaker or a campaign event (CL-002, CL-005) more than as the power to remove; an option that may change more could seat that figure at a desk, but not with CL-026's red tie.
  - A gavel or scales, which would say the court is the subject (R-11, R-16, CL-027).
  - A shield or padlock, which the inventory rules out and R-12 already uses.
  - A figure being pushed out a door, which is R-10 Revolving-Door Limits.

### Testify (new Act drawing, DR-141)

- **V7 name and ID.** Not in V7. The nearest V7 elements:
  - issue I047, "Residents with less time and money have less say between elections", which names hearings, public comments and meetings
  - pathway PW15, "Voting and public influence"
  - reform R-33, Citizen Assemblies
- **Meaning, from our own copy.** Chapter 01 (Rule 01.13's why) says a reader "can call an office or testify before it". The tracker's step 2 copy (D2-423) says many state legislatures take testimony from the public, and congressional committees hear the witnesses they invite. In one sentence: a member of the public speaks for the record at a public hearing, before a body such as a state legislative committee, a city council or a regulatory board.
- **What a reader must recognize.** One ordinary person speaking, with a microphone, at a witness table, facing a panel of several officials who sit together at a raised bench, often a curved dais. Legislatures' own guides describe this scene. Nebraska: a witness fills out "the sign-in sheet at the witness table" ("Tips on Testifying at a Committee Hearing", nebraskalegislature.gov). Minnesota: "you should be seated at the testifiers' table" and "Speak slowly and clearly into the microphone" (Minnesota House, "Committees" brochure, February 2025). Washington's guide adds: "Legislative hearings are conducted informally. They are not judicial proceedings" (leg.wa.gov, "How to testify in committee", 2023), which is one more reason to keep court signs out (section 4 has the full citations). The direction matters: the person speaks to the officials, and the officials sit above and listen. DR-147's option says this. Red on the microphone or on the speaker's sound marks would put the second sign on the act of speaking.
- **What would mislead.**
  - One figure at a raised bench, a gavel, scales or a robe. Those read as a courtroom, where a witness testifies in a trial (CL-027, R-11, R-16). Show at least three officials and no gavel.
  - A raised right hand, which reads as a court oath or as voting yes.
  - A lectern facing a seated audience, which is CL-005 Public Meeting and reads as a speech.
  - Two people level across a table, which is CL-016 Lobbying.
  - Flags behind the dais (Rule 01.6), and nameplates with party letters (Rule 01.7).

### Contact an official (new Act drawing, DR-141)

- **V7 name and ID.** Not in V7. The nearest V7 elements:
  - pathway PW15, "Voting and public influence", whose short answer says randomized experiments in two state legislatures found that constituent calls and emails about a specific bill changed legislators' votes (V7's claim, quoted as V7 states it, not checked here)
  - V7's sources K1723, House of Representatives, "Find Your Representative", https://www.house.gov/representatives/find-your-representative, and K1724, United States Senate, "Contacting U.S. Senators", https://www.senate.gov/senators/senators-contact.htm (both recorded by V7 as checked 2026-10-15)
- **Meaning, from our own copy.** Chapter 01 says a reader "can call an office". In one sentence: a reader calls, emails, writes to or visits the office of an official who represents them, to ask for help or to say how they want that official to act.
- **What a reader must recognize.** A person, an official or an office, and a message going from the person to the office. Washington's legislature lists the channels in one line: "writing a letter, sending an e-mail, calling the legislator's Olympia office, or by calling the Legislative Hotline" (leg.wa.gov, "How to testify in committee", 2023). The channel can be shown (a phone, a letter), but the drawing should say "to an official", not just "make a call".
- **What would mislead.**
  - A phone handset alone. It is the common interface sign for "call", so it may match a utility glyph (the accessibility audit sets that list) and fail the shape-likeness check. Dustin asked for a very simple phone (DR-147). If the utility set has a phone, the topic phone needs one extra part, such as the red sound marks or a small office, and the board should show the pair side by side.
  - Person, arrow and envelope, which is CL-061 Household Income. An envelope also reads as our newsletter's subscribe action.
  - Two speech bubbles (R-12, CL-008). One red speech mark on the line between person and desk, as DR-147 describes, is safe.
  - An official at a desk with a red tie, which is CL-026 Executive Power and reads as the President.
  - A megaphone or a placard, which reads as protest (Rule 01.7).
  - A hand pressing a button, which is CL-022 Act, the pillar icon.

## 2. Civic Line labels against Atlas V7 names and IDs

### How I checked

I compared every label in `work/build/assets/civic-line/20261029_Civic-Line-Inventory.csv` (122 symbols: CL-001 to CL-072 and R-01 to R-50) by script against three records of V7:

- **V7 titles and IDs** from the atlas editorial data (51 variables, 50 reforms).

I confirmed all 25 that sit on a loop against the loops' `chain_labels` in the editorial data, with no difference. ### A. A federal records request (Freedom of Information Act)

Sources, all read in full and retrieved November 9, 2026:
- **The statute.** 5 U.S.C. 552, via Cornell Law School's Legal Information Institute, https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/5/552.
- **The government's FOIA site.** FOIA.gov, "Frequently Asked Questions", https://www.foia.gov/faq.html, run by the Justice Department's Office of Information Policy.
- **The Justice Department's own report.** "Fiscal Year 2024 Annual Freedom of Information Act Report", Office of Information Policy, March 6, 2025, https://www.justice.gov/oip/media/1392121/dl. Its section III defines the outcome words, and its tables V.B(1) and VI.B count requests and appeals by them.

**Proposed steps (six).**

| Step | Plain name | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Requested | The request reaches the agency's FOIA office, in writing, describing the records. The agency's 20 working days start | FOIA.gov FAQ: "The request simply must be in writing and reasonably describe the records you seek"; 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(A)(i): "determine within 20 days (excepting Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays) after the receipt of any such request whether to comply" |
| 2 | Acknowledged | The agency confirms receipt and gives a tracking number | FOIA.gov FAQ: "you will usually receive a letter acknowledging the request with an assigned tracking number"; 552(a)(7)(A) requires a tracking number for "each request received that will take longer than ten days to process" |
| 3 | Searching | The agency looks for the records | FOIA.gov FAQ: "The agency will typically search for records in response to your request" |
| 4 | Reviewing | The agency decides what can be released and blacks out what an exemption protects | FOIA.gov FAQ: it will "review those records to determine which - and what parts of each - can be released. The agency will redact, or black out, any information protected from disclosure by one of the FOIA's nine exemptions" |
| 5 | Answered | The agency sends its decision and the releasable records | FOIA.gov FAQ: "Once the agency has processed your request it will send you a written response"; 552(a)(6)(A)(i)(I) |
| 6 | Appeal | Only if the requester appeals. The agency's appeal office reviews the answer and decides | 552(a)(6)(A)(i)(III)(aa): the right to appeal "within a period ... that is not less than 90 days after the date of such adverse determination"; 552(a)(6)(A)(ii): a decision on an appeal "within twenty days (excepting Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays)". DOJ report, definition a: an appeal asks the agency to "review at a higher administrative level a FOIA determination" |

A request that is never appealed ends at step 5, and step 6 takes a slash. For scale, at the Justice Department alone in fiscal year 2024 (October 2023 to September 2024), 2,477 administrative appeals were processed and 157,180 requests were processed (annual report, tables VI.B and V.B(1)). An appeal in that year may concern a request from an earlier year, so the two numbers give a rough scale, not a rate. Steps 3 and 4 can merge into one, "Processing", if the tracker needs five; FOIA.gov describes them as one sentence and the DOJ report counts neither separately.

**Pauses a status line may need.** An agency may stop the 20-day clock once to ask the requester for information, and to settle fees (552(a)(6)(A), the two tolling clauses). It may extend the deadline by up to ten working days "in unusual circumstances" with written notice (552(a)(6)(B)(i)). These are waits inside a step, so I would show them in words ("Waiting on you: the agency asked a question") and not as steps.

**End states after step 5, in the DOJ report's own categories (section III and table V.B(1)).**

| Plain word | Official category | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| Granted | Full Grant | "an agency decision to disclose all records in full in response to a FOIA request" |
| Partly granted | Partial Grant/Partial Denial | "an agency decision to disclose portions of the records and to withhold other portions that are exempt under the FOIA, or to otherwise deny a portion of the request for a procedural reason" |
| Denied | Full Denial (based on exemptions) | "an agency decision not to release any records in response to a FOIA request because the records are exempt in their entireties under one or more of the FOIA exemptions" |
| Closed, no records released | Full Denial based on reasons other than exemptions | The DOJ table lists these reasons: no records; all records referred to another component or agency; request withdrawn; fee-related reason; records not reasonably described; improper FOIA request for other reason; not agency record; duplicate request; other |
| Withdrawn | Request Withdrawn | One of the reasons above. The government counts a withdrawn request as a full denial for a reason other than exemptions; a reader page should say "Withdrawn by the requester" so it does not read as a refusal |

**End states after step 6, the appeal (DOJ table VI.B).** Upheld ("Affirmed on Appeal"); Partly reversed ("Partially Affirmed & Partially Reversed/Remanded"); Reversed or sent back ("Completely Reversed/Remanded"); Closed for other reasons.

**After the agency.** Two routes lie outside the agency and outside the six steps: mediation by the Office of Government Information Services at the National Archives, "a nonexclusive alternative to litigation" (552(h)(3)), and a lawsuit in federal district court (552(a)(4)(B)). FOIA.gov describes both. I would show either in words after the end state ("Taken to court, March 2027") and not draw a seventh step.

**On the brief's word list.** "Appealed" is a step and not an end state: an appeal ends in one of the four outcomes above. "Granted", "partly granted", "denied" and "withdrawn" all hold as end states.

| Step | Plain name | What happens | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Early notice | Optional.
