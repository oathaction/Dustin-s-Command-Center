## 1. H.R. 7008, the Stop Insider Trading Act

### What the record shows

- **Name.** The bill's short title is "Stop Insider Trading Act" in every version. Its official title, as introduced and as passed by the House, reads "To amend chapter 131 of title 5 to require certain restrictions on stocks for Members of Congress and their spouses and dependents, and for other purposes." Rep. Bryan Steil of Wisconsin introduced it on January 12, 2026, with 93 cosponsors listed.
- **The stock section (section 2).** It adds a new subchapter to title 5. Its core sentence: "no covered individual may purchase a covered investment." A covered individual is a member of Congress, a spouse or a dependent child. A covered investment is "a security issued by a publicly traded company or any comparable economic interest acquired through synthetic means, such as the use of a derivative". Widely held investment funds, small business interests and trusts the member does not control are excluded. Selling is allowed after a public notice filed 7 to 14 days before the sale, so nobody has to sell what they already own. A violation costs the greater of $2,000 or 10 percent of the transaction, plus any net gain, and an improper purchase must be sold. The fee may not come from campaign money or office allowances. The section takes effect 180 days after enactment.
- **The photo ID section (section 3).** Its heading is "Requiring voters to provide photo identification". It amends the Help America Vote Act of 2002 with a new section 303A, "Photo identification requirements". An in-person voter in a federal election must show "a valid physical photo identification"; without one, the voter casts a provisional ballot and has three days to show the ID or sign a religious-objection affidavit. A mail voter sends a copy of a photo ID, or the last four digits of a Social Security number with an affidavit saying the voter could not obtain one. Absent uniformed services voters and some elderly and disabled voters are exempt. Accepted IDs are a state driver's license or ID card, a U.S. passport, a Defense or Veterans Affairs photo ID, or a tribal photo ID. The section takes effect 90 days after enactment.
- **When the photo ID section entered.** It is absent from the text as introduced (January 12) and as reported by the Committee on House Administration (February 3). It is present in the text as passed by the House (July 22) and as placed on the Senate calendar (August 6). The House took the bill up under H. Res. 1438, whose section 8 treated as adopted a substitute made of "Rules Committee Print 119–38, modified by the amendment printed in part B of the report of the Committee on Rules". I did not open the Rules Committee print or report, so I cannot say which of the two added section 3.
- **The official summary is out of date for this point.** The Congressional Research Service summary in the bill status file describes the bill as reported on February 3. It says the bill "generally prohibits Members of Congress and their spouses and dependent children from purchasing stocks and requires public notice before these individuals may sell stocks", and it does not mention photo ID, because that version had none.
- **House vote.** Roll Call 280, July 22, 2026, on passage: passed 232 to 198, one not voting. Republicans 218 yes and 0 no; Democrats 13 yes, 198 no and 1 not voting; one member the Clerk lists as independent, Kiley of California, voted yes. Before passage, a Democratic motion to recommit failed 211 to 218 (Roll Call 279).
- **Senate vote.** Vote 253, September 30, 2026, on cloture on the motion to proceed to Calendar No. 548, H.R. 7008: 53 yeas, 47 nays. The presiding officer ruled: "Three-fifths of the Senators duly chosen and sworn not having voted in the affirmative, the motion is not agreed to." The Congressional Record prints all 100 names on page S5199. Matched against a roster of current senators, the 53 yeas are the Senate's 53 Republicans, and the 47 nays are its 45 Democrats and its two independents, King and Sanders. The atlas's 53 to 47 is correct.
- **Since then.** The bill status file, updated October 1, 2026, lists no action after September 30. The Senate's Daily Digest for September 30 shows the Senate in pro forma sessions until November 9.
- **The atlas.** Issue I043's status text already names the photo ID title ("it carries a separate title requiring photo identification to vote in federal elections"). Reform R-09's status text and its who-decides paragraph call the bill "a ban on stock trading" and leave the photo ID section out. The brand report's line that "the atlas status text omits it too" holds for R-09 only. R-09 belongs on the V7.1 issue list.

## 2. The 23-state initiative count and Rule 01.5's source line

### What the record shows

- **The count.** The studio's file `work/boards/phase-2/data-viz/shared/public/ballotpedia-citizen-initiative-states.csv` records Ballotpedia's page "States with initiative or referendum" (revision 11698211), read earlier on November 6, 2026: 24 states have a statewide citizen initiative, 23 can use it, and DC has its own process. I could not reopen the page today (Ballotpedia's server returned HTTP 403), and NCSL's page refused as well (HTTP 403 from Cloudflare). The count therefore rests on the earlier read of a secondary source.
- **Florida.** Article XI, section 3 of the Florida Constitution, read on flsenate.gov: "The power to propose the revision or amendment of any portion or portions of this constitution by initiative is reserved to the people, provided that, any such revision or amendment, except for those limiting the power of government to raise revenue, shall embrace but one subject and matter directly connected therewith." Article XI holds no route for a citizen-initiated statute. Section 5(e) adds the approval threshold: an amendment takes effect "if the proposed amendment or revision is approved by vote of at least sixty percent of the electors voting on the measure".
- **Illinois.** Article XIV, section 3 of the Illinois Constitution, as quoted by the Illinois Appellate Court in Clark v. Illinois State Board of Elections, 2014 IL App (1st) 141937 (Illinois Official Reports, illinoiscourts.gov): "Amendments to Article IV of this Constitution may be proposed by a petition signed by a number of electors equal in number to at least eight percent of the total votes cast for candidates for Governor in the preceding gubernatorial election. Amendments shall be limited to structural and procedural subjects contained in Article IV." Article IV is the legislative article. The amendment passes with three-fifths of those voting on it or a majority of those voting in the election. I could not open the Illinois General Assembly's copy of the constitution (ilga.gov failed its certificate check through this session's proxy and returned HTTP 503 to the fetch tool). Search results show resolutions to remove the limit in the 100th and 103rd General Assemblies; I found nothing showing one passed.
- **Mississippi.** The Mississippi Supreme Court, In re Initiative Measure No. 65: Mayor Mary Hawkins Butler v. Michael Watson, No. 2020-IA-01199-SCT, decided May 14, 2021 (courts.ms.gov, opened). The court quotes Article 15, section 273(3): "The people reserve unto themselves the power to propose and enact constitutional amendments by initiative", and the rule that signatures "from any congressional district shall not exceed one-fifth (1/5) of the total number of signatures required". Its holding: "Drafted as it is, section 273(3) disables itself in being unworkable with fewer than five congressional districts in Mississippi." Mississippi has had four districts since the 2000 census. Its process covers constitutional amendments only.
- **Mississippi in 2026.** Official-domain search results show several 2026 resolutions to restore the process (Senate Concurrent Resolutions 515, 517 and 519; House Concurrent Resolutions 3, 20 and 40), each as introduced. News results report that the Senate tabled one on February 12, 2026 and that others died in committee, and Ballotpedia's 2026 page (snippet) listed no Mississippi measure for November 3, 2026. I could not open the Legislature's bill status pages (certificate failure through the proxy, then HTTP 503), the Secretary of State's site (HTTP 403) or Mississippi Today (HTTP 403). That the process is still unusable today is therefore secondary.

## 5. How the NBER paper and EPI describe their authors

### What the sources say

- **The NBER paper.** NBER Working Paper 24165, "Productivity and Pay: Is the link broken?", issue date December 2017, DOI 10.3386/w24165, opened on nber.org. The page lists "Anna M. Stansbury & Lawrence H. Summers". The paper's title page gives their affiliations: Stansbury, "Department of Economics, Harvard University"; Summers, "Harvard Kennedy School of Government ... and NBER". The page describes the publisher this way: "Founded in 1920, the NBER is a private, non-profit, non-partisan organization dedicated to conducting economic research". The paper notes that NBER working papers "have not been peer-reviewed".
- **The paper's finding.** From its abstract: "over 1973-2016, one percentage point higher productivity growth has been associated with 0.7 to 1 percentage points higher median and average compensation growth and with 0.4 to 0.7 percentage points higher production/nonsupervisory compensation growth." It also says "Since 1973 median compensation has diverged starkly from average labor productivity." The finding covers median (typical) pay as well as average pay. The atlas's connection LE04 reports the estimate as "average pay growth" only, and the guide's specimen follows it; both understate the paper.
- **EPI.** epi.org refused this session (HTTP 403 from Cloudflare), so everything here is from search results on epi.org. EPI's standard description, as the snippets give it: "The Economic Policy Institute (EPI) is a nonprofit, nonpartisan think tank created in 1986 to include the needs of low- and middle-income workers, women workers, and workers of color in economic policy discussions." The snippets show no byline on "The Productivity-Pay Gap" page itself; other EPI pieces credit Lawrence Mishel and Josh Bivens for the original gap chart. ## 6. DR-99: a mechanism for "Our democracy is broken."

### Massachusetts Question 3's subject

Question 3 on the November 3, 2026 Massachusetts ballot is a proposed law about primary elections. The Secretary of the Commonwealth's Information for Voters, as official-domain search results quote it: "A YES VOTE would eliminate separate party primaries for state elections and require a single primary in which all candidates appear on one ballot, voters could vote for any candidate, and the top two candidates would advance to the general election." The atlas adds that it covers state and congressional offices, lets a party's endorsement appear on the ballot, and would take effect January 1, 2028 (reform R-01). Its subject is how candidates are nominated: one all-candidate primary with the top two advancing. The Secretary's pages sent this session into a redirect loop, so I read them only as search results. I did not check the result of the vote.

### Three candidates

None touches ranked-choice voting, primaries, nominations or Question 3's subject. Each comes from the atlas export (`work/boards/phase-2/atlas-editorial/shared/20261105_Atlas-Editorial-Data.json`, snapshot of October 15, 2026). W07's line about low-turnout primaries is out under DR-99.

**First choice: connection L50, "Partisan loyalty shields incumbents from voter punishment" (Loop R11, the party-loyalty loop). Grade: Well supported.** The atlas's plain line: "Strong partisans rarely vote against their own party's candidate, even one who breaks democratic norms, so the threat of losing shrinks." Evidence: Graham and Svolik (2020), survey experiments with 1,691 U.S. respondents; about 3.5 percent left their own party's candidate in realistic matchups, and the average penalty across all matchups was 11.7 points. It explains the claim directly: officials can break the rules a democracy runs on because their own voters rarely punish them, in either party.

> The mechanism: "Few voters leave their own party's candidate over an undemocratic stand, so officials who take one face little threat of losing office."

> Source: Oath & Action's systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, connection L50, partisan loyalty and the risk of losing office, snapshot of October 15, 2026, United States. Retrieved November 6, 2026. From survey experiments with 1,691 respondents (Graham and Svolik, 2020); about 3.5 percent left their own party's candidate in realistic matchups.

Keep the line to L50. The loop's own qualification adds that it "bites hardest where seats are safe", which leads toward safe seats and primaries.

**Second choice: connection L26, "Local reporting raises pressure on incumbents" (the first step of Loop R16, the news-desert loop). Grade: Well supported for this connection; the loop as a whole is a hypothesis, so cite the connection.** The atlas's plain line: "Coverage of local officials draws more challengers and more voters, which narrows the advantage incumbents hold." The evidence is from U.S. mayoral elections (Rubado and Jennings, 2020). It explains the claim at the level most readers live: where nobody covers city hall, fewer people run and fewer vote.

> The mechanism: "Where local news covers city hall, more challengers run for mayor and more residents vote, so incumbents face a real contest."

> Source: Oath & Action's systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, connection L26, local reporting and the risk of losing office, snapshot of October 15, 2026, United States. Retrieved November 6, 2026. The evidence comes from U.S. mayoral elections (Rubado and Jennings, 2020).

**Third choice: connection L11, "A weaker Congress invites presidential action" (Loop R13, the executive-power loop). Grade: Well supported (Howell, 2003).** The atlas's plain line: "When Congress does not legislate, presidents fill the gap with executive orders, emergency declarations, tariffs and spending freezes." It explains why voters feel their representatives decide less. It names presidents in general; the atlas's case for this loop cites the current administration's tariffs and removals, so a piece that uses it must keep the sentence general or give cases from presidents of both parties under Rule 01.11.

> The mechanism: "When Congress does not pass laws, presidents fill the gap with executive orders, emergency declarations, tariffs and spending freezes."

> Source: Oath & Action's systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, connection L11, Congress's capacity and presidential action, snapshot of October 15, 2026, United States. Retrieved November 6, 2026. The evidence is studies of presidential power (Howell, 2003); the return step, that such action weakens Congress further, is graded plausible.

My recommendation is L50, because it names the step at which accountability fails, and it applies to both parties' voters in the same words.
