# Stretch test red team: statistics and nonpartisanship

Oath & Action Brand Guide, Edition 06. Phase 2, November 6, 2026. Reviewer 1 of the stretch test review brief (`work/handoffs/phase-2/20261106_Data-Viz-Stretch-Review-Brief.md`). Paths are relative to `oath-and-action-v6-brand-guide/`.

The page under review is `work/boards/phase-2/data-viz/stretch/20261106_Data-Viz-Stretch.html` (views `#newsprint`, `#dark`, `#phone`), built by `stretch/build_stretch.py`. I rebuilt all four stretch datasets from the public CSVs, recomputed every number in every title and note, measured every drawn scale from the SVG markup, rendered every view with Playwright, and read every title, note, label and ordering once as a reader on the left and once as a reader on the right. Quotations are copied from the page. Titles are studio drafts, and so are the replacement titles I suggest.

## 1. Verdict

**The page is not ready for Board 4 as built, and one fix pass will clear it.** Every number on it traces to the shared data, and the shared data matches the CSVs row for row. Party marks are handled with real care. Five things still mislead a reader. One is a title the chart contradicts (S16), one an arrow whose length does not follow the number (S12), one an ordering label in a different unit from the figures printed under it (S13), one a phone card that drops the words for direction (S15) and one a pair of ranked lists the survey cannot support (S16). Each fix stays inside one chart and needs no new data.

**Statistics: 3 of 5.** S11, S13, S14, S15 and the S16 circle areas are drawn exactly, and the qualifications for survey error, preliminary months and fusion voting are all on the page. S12's arrows compress the shift, though, and two titles and one label say more than the data holds.

**Nonpartisanship: 4 of 5.** The mechanics hold on every view: neutral inks, party names in words, no red, no Prussian, no pair B, symmetric sort messages and no administration named. No title leans in its words. What keeps it from a 5: the phone cards lose the words for direction, S16's highest and lowest lists sort the two parties' states into best and worst on noise, and the three election titles repeat one finding.

## 2. Findings, ranked

Severity follows the brief. Must fix: a reader would be misled, blocked or excluded, or a locked rule is broken. Should fix: a clear loss of quality or balance. Polish: small.

### Must fix

**ST-M1. S16, every view (Newsprint, Charcoal, Phones) and every artboard. The title is contradicted by the chart under it.** The title reads "Three of the four states with the most eligible voters, Texas, Florida and New York, reported turnout below the national rate of 65.3 percent." California is the fourth, and its reported rate, 64.7 percent, is also below 65.3. The chart prints "California 25.3 million, 64.7%" a short way under "Nation: 65.3%". The shading agrees: California sits in the "60 to 64.9" step, one step lighter than the step that holds the national rate. "Three of the four" comes from a test the title never names. The note does name it: "Texas, Florida and New York each sit below the national rate by more than their margins of error; California's 64.7 percent is within its margin of the nation's 65.3." That test holds: Texas sits 7.4 points below the nation against a margin of 2.3, Florida 5.0 against 1.9, New York 6.1 against 2.6. Why it matters: the title is the sentence a reader repeats, and anyone who checks it against the chart finds it wrong. Fix: put the test in the title, for example "Texas, Florida and New York, three of the four states with the most citizens 18 and over, reported turnout below the national 65.3 percent by more than the survey's margin of error." Keep the note.

**ST-M2. S12, Newsprint and Phones, all four artboards and the size key. The arrow's length does not follow the shift.** The build makes each arrow's horizontal run exact (6.06 px per point on the wide map; I measured 6.056 to 6.061). It then tilts each arrow from 62 degrees for a small shift to 22 degrees at 12 points (`tile_arrow` tokens). What a reader sees is the slanted arrow, and its length is not proportional. Measured from the 1,024 px SVG, Washington's 0.98-point shift draws 11.4 px long and New York's 10.54-point shift 71.6 px. That is 6.3 times as long for a shift 10.8 times as large, and the 600 px map gives the same ratio. The key repeats the distortion: its "10 points" arrow is 69.0 px and its "5 points" arrow 43.1 px, 1.6 times as long. The rise is not monotonic either, so New York's arrow climbs less than Florida's (32.3 px against 33.4 px). Small shifts point mostly up (Washington's run is 5.9 px and its rise 9.8 px), while the drawing says "All 51 arrows point right, toward the Republican nominee." The rendered check passes because it measures the run alone (`data-axis="x"`). The note asks the reader to do the same: "Each arrow's run across its square is the same number of pixels for each point of shift, so arrows compare across the map." Readers judge the slanted line. Why it matters: the brief asks whether the arrow's length is honest. Here a reader comparing two states by eye takes in about 60 percent of the true ratio at the top of the range, on the chart whose question is where the shift happened. Fix: draw every tile arrow flat, pointing right. Its length then equals its run, it points the way S11's axis and the note say, and it loses the upward "rising" cue. A flat 10.54-point arrow at 6.06 px per point is 64 px, which fits the 80 px tile. Keep `data-axis="x"` on the shaft. Retire `angle_small`, `angle_large` and their phone pair, and drop the sentence "The angle flattens as the shift grows, a second cue to the same number."

**ST-M3. S15, Newsprint and Phones, whenever the table is under 640 px wide (every phone, and the Phones view at every width). The cards drop the words for direction.** Under 640 px the cards hide the bars and the center line (`.mb{display:none}`, `.subhead{display:none}`). The only words for the shift sit in the column header, "Shift toward the Republican nominee", which scrolls away after the first card. Only the studio strip is sticky. Each card then reads, for the District of Columbia, "2024 Democratic +83.8", "2020 Democratic +86.8" and "+2.9" beside a right-pointing arrow. In one card "+" means "lead" twice and then "toward the Republican nominee" once, and nothing says so. A reader who scrolls to Minnesota reads "Democratic +4.2", "Democratic +7.1" and "+2.9", and can take the last as the Democratic lead growing by 2.9 points when it shrank by that much. Why it matters: DR-58 says arrows, position and words carry direction. On the cards, position is gone and the words are off screen, so the direction rests on an unlabeled arrow whose "right" no longer points at anything. Fix: print words in each card's shift cell, such as "2.9 toward the Republican nominee", and keep the arrow. The interface reviewer should also check that each cell keeps its header for screen readers once the table is set `display:block`. I did not run a screen reader.

**ST-M4. S16, every view and artboard. The "HIGHEST RATES" and "LOWEST RATES" lists rank states the survey cannot rank, and the cut sorts by party.** The chart prints "HIGHEST RATES DC 79.5% Minnesota 75.9% Oregon 75.3%" and "LOWEST RATES Arkansas 52.8% Texas 57.9% Louisiana 58.0%". The gaps sit inside the survey's error. Texas and Louisiana differ by 0.1 point against a combined 90 percent margin of about 4.3 points (the root sum of squares of 2.3 and 3.6). Louisiana and Hawaii (58.5, ±3.4) differ by 0.5 against 5.0, and New York (59.2, ±2.6) sits 1.2 points above Louisiana against 4.4. Oregon and Virginia (72.9, ±2.4) differ by 2.4 against 3.7. The chart's own note says "a gap of a few points between two states may not be real." The three states listed as highest were all led by the Democratic nominee in 2024, and the three listed as lowest by the Republican nominee. Hawaii and New York, both led by the Democratic nominee, sit in the same noise as Louisiana and miss the cut. Why it matters: a reader on the right sees a list that ranks the parties' states best and worst on differences the survey does not support, and a careful reader of either side sees the chart break its own warning. Fix: replace both lists with the range and its margins, for example "Range: Arkansas 52.8% (±2.7) to DC 79.5% (±3.3). Gaps of a few points between states may not be real." The step key already names every state by band, and its "Under 60%" step holds nine states, seven led by the Republican nominee and two by the Democratic nominee.

**ST-M5. S13, Newsprint and Phones, every artboard. "Largest gain first" orders by percent, while every panel prints jobs.** The eyebrow reads "PRIVATE INDUSTRIES, LARGEST GAIN FIRST". The build sorts by percent change (`sorted(PRIVATE, key=lambda s_: -last[s_]['pct_change_since_2022_01'])`), but each panel prints only its change in jobs. So the printed figures run "+3.8 million", "+61,000", "+1.6 million", "+769,000", "+338,000", "+436,000", "+29,000", and Utilities (+61,000, 11th of 14 by jobs) sits second, ahead of leisure and hospitality (+1.6 million). Why it matters: the brief names this confusion, percent change against level change. A reader sets "largest gain first" against the only numbers printed and comes away believing utilities had the second-largest gain. Fix: name the order's unit ("PRIVATE INDUSTRIES, LARGEST PERCENT GAIN FIRST") and print each panel's percent beside its jobs figure ("+61,000, +11.1%"), as the totals rows already do ("+9.0 million", "+6.0%").

**ST-S2. S14, every view. The title says "job gains" and states a preliminary figure bare.** The title reads "The three-month average of monthly job gains fell from 279,000 in January 2023 to 51,000 in September 2026, and payrolls shrank in seven months." The average fell below zero in five months (August, October, November and December 2025, and February 2026; lowest −44,700 in October 2025), so it averages changes, which include losses. The end figure rests on two preliminary months. September's average takes in July, August and September, and only July is settled. The last average with no preliminary month, July 2026, is 28,000. The chart dashes that stretch and the table marks it, but the title does not. Why it matters: the title is what travels, and the September figure will move. **ST-S3. S13 and S14, every view. The two jobs charts start a year apart and neither says why.** S13 measures change "since January 2022". S14 runs "January 2023 to September 2026". The source line under both says "January 2021 to September 2026". The base decides the story. Federal jobs show −197,000 since January 2022, but they rose 130,000 to December 2024 and then fell 328,000 from January 2025. S14's title starts at 279,000, the highest average inside its window. The brief asks for January 2022 to be explained, and an unexplained start date is the first thing a suspicious reader on either side checks. Fix: the main session picks the reason, or one shared start for both charts, since the windows come from the shared data's value field and required keys. The maker then prints that reason in one line in each measure line.

**ST-S4. S16, every view, and its studio strip. "Eligible voters" claims more than the survey measures.** The title says "the most eligible voters". Fix: use "citizens 18 and over" in the title and the strip (ST-M1's suggested title already does).

**ST-S5. S11, S12 and S15 shift figures, desktop. The plus sign makes "toward the Republican nominee" the positive direction.** Every shift prints with a plus ("+10.5", "NY +10.5"). Under the data's Republican-minus-Democratic convention, the sign means "toward the Republican nominee". On S15 the same "+" means a lead for either party one cell to the left ("Democratic +83.8 … +2.9"). Every shift runs one way, and the arrows and the words already say which, so the sign adds a valence and no information. **ST-S6. S12, every view. The chart that leads with New York does not explain New York's count.** The title names New York first. New York's figures are the only ones that count fused party lines, and S12 says so only in the source line's "New York counts fused party lines", a term the chart never explains. **ST-S7. S12, Phones view and Newsprint under 600 px. The stretch brief allowed this ("decide what the phone map shows and what moves to a list under it").

## 3. Nonpartisanship, read from both sides

**Inks and red (DR-48, DR-58, Rule 05.3).** Every arrow, bar, frame and label on S11, S12 and S15 is Charcoal or Stone. S15 sets 2024 in Charcoal and 2020 in Stone, which separates years, not parties. My scan of every hex color in the source found Pillar Red (#E63946) only as the `--red` token on the three grounds and in the two wordmark ampersands, and no Prussian anywhere. My run of the rendered check counted one red mark per view (the wordmark, under `data-red="signature"`) and no working-red job on Newsprint, Charcoal or Phones, so Rule 05.3 holds on every view. It counted 1,185 marks under `data-party` on Newsprint and Phones, none painted Pillar Red or Prussian.

**Pair B.** It appears nowhere. Verdigris appears only as S16's one-sided turnout ramp, which no reader could take for a party pair.

**Order and position.** S11 sorts by the 2024 margin, with the Democratic side on the left and the Republican side on the right, alphabetical and the usual left-right order. S15 opens on the same order and reverses it on request. Its status lines are symmetric: "Sorted by 2024 margin, largest Democratic lead first." and "…largest Republican lead first."

**Titles, once from the left and once from the right.**
- S11. Left: a plain record of a loss, with no adjective, and the smallest shift is named too. Right: a plain record of a gain, with no claim of a mandate. No lean in the words. The repetition across three titles is ST-S1.
- S12. Left: three of the four named states were led by the Democratic nominee in both years, so the title reads as erosion. Right: it reads as a trend. It gives no cause. No lean.
- S13. Left: "the federal government lost jobs" reads as cuts. Right: it reads as smaller government, and health care's gain leads the sentence. The title gives no cause and names no administration. The federal figure is a net over a window in which federal jobs rose and then fell (ST-S3, ST-P6). No lean.
- S14. Left: "payrolls shrank in seven months", with the drawing's note dating all seven to 2025 and 2026, reads as blame. Right: the average's fall begins in 2023, which the chart draws, and the April 2024 ring marks that the average stopped reaching 150,000 before 2025. The two answer each other. The title names no cause or administration. Its faults are statistical (ST-S2).
- S15. Both sides read counts of states. No lean in the words. Repetition (ST-S1).
- S16. The title names two states led by the Republican nominee and one led by the Democratic nominee. No lean. The lists under the map do lean (ST-M4).

**The jobs pair.** S13 and S14 read as neither praise nor blame. Neither names an administration or a policy, and neither gives a cause.

**The page as a whole (DR-55).** The page holds a finding each side would rather skip, so DR-55 is met. The weight is uneven: three election titles carry one message, and the right's counterweight sits in one S14 note. ST-S1 evens it.
