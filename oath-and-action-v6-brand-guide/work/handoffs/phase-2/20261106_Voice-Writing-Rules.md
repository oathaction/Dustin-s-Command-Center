# How Oath & Action writes

Chapter 02 Voice, Edition 06. Draft for Board 5, November 6, 2026, revised after the creative director's review.

*These 21 rules teach writing that sounds like a person talking, and each one shows a sentence before and after the fix. Ten house rules for every page come with them.*

Our tagline is “A country that answers to its people.” People can hold an office to account once they know which office decided. So we name who acts, in words you would use at a kitchen table, as in “A regulator or a board approves your rate.”

Our reader promise is “We explain how a decision is made and give you a practical way to take part.” A reader can take part only in a decision they can follow, which is why clarity comes first in our editorial commitment, “Clarity is our duty. Action is our promise.”

Our founder and editor, Dustin Cathcart, set these rules for everything Oath & Action publishes. Sixteen of the 21 rules quote our own work and rewrite the line to pass every rule on the page. Where we found none, we wrote one, and anything we invented carries the Illustrative label.

## 1. Sound like a person talking

Write the sentence you would say across a kitchen table, with the actor up front and a rhythm that keeps changing.

### W01 Write it the way you would say it

Write the way you would explain something to a neighbor, in everyday words and full sentences. Read the draft aloud and rewrite any line you would not say.

*Terms.* Plain prose: everyday words in complete sentences, with nothing a listener would need to see on the page to follow.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, the sample research brief in its Reports chapter)

> ~~Make the point of responsibility visible~~.

**After**

> **Show the reader which office answers for the decision**.

What changed: The rewrite names the office, the thing a reader would ask about, and read aloud it sounds like one person talking to another.

### W02 Name who acts

Put the person or office that acts in front of the verb. When you find a passive verb, ask who does the action and name them.

*Terms.* Active voice: a sentence that names the actor first, as in “The council passed the budget.” Passive voice puts the thing acted on first and often hides the actor, as in “The budget was passed.”

**Before** (Our systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, the everyday answer to “Why is my electric bill going up?”)

> Your rate ~~is approved by~~ a regulator or a board.

**After**

> A regulator or a board **approves** your rate.

What changed: The rewrite names who decides in its first five words, so a listener hears the answer before anything else.

### W03 Change the length of your sentences

Mix short sentences with long ones, and keep the mix hard to predict. A run of sentences the same size starts to sound like a metronome.

**Before** (Our systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, reform profile “Rules for drug-pricing middlemen (PBMs)”, how it would be carried out)

> The ~~FTC~~ first finalizes ~~its pending consent orders~~. The federal statute's provisions take effect in 2028. Audits then check whether savings pass through to patients and plans.

**After**

> The **Federal Trade Commission** first finalizes **the consent orders it has pending, court-approved settlements that bind a company for a set term**. The federal statute’s provisions take effect in 2028. Audits then check whether savings pass through to patients and plans.

What changed: The rewrite defines consent orders in one long sentence ahead of two shorter ones, so the voice rises and falls and a newcomer can follow.

### W04 Join thoughts that belong together

Use the small words that show how thoughts connect, such as because, so, when and once. Save short sentences for the moments that need one.

*Terms.* Parataxis: setting clauses or sentences side by side with no words to link them, which as a rhythm device chops one thought into short beats.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 02 Voice, sample lines)

> Know the rules~~. Find~~ the lever.

**After**

> **Once you** know the rules, **you can find** the lever**, a place where one change has wide effects**.

What changed: “Once” tells a listener that finding the lever depends on knowing the rules, and the rewrite defines the lever in the atlas glossary’s words.

## 2. Put the point first and stop

Open with what the reader came for, and end when the facts run out.

### W05 Open with something the reader can use

Make the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step. Cut lines that only announce what comes next, and stock phrases that lean on the words after them.

*Terms.* Throat-clearing opener: a sentence that announces what is coming in place of saying it. Rhetorical crutch: a stock phrase that props up a sentence and adds no meaning, such as “the truth is” or “make no mistake”.

**Before** (An early draft of this guide, the headline of a mock article on taking part. The draft is real, and everything it describes is invented.)

> Alder County votes on backyard cottages March 9. ~~Here is how to weigh in.~~ *Illustrative*

**After**

> Alder County votes on backyard cottages March 9. **Send a comment by March 3 or speak at the meeting.** *Illustrative*

What changed: Read aloud, the rewrite gives the deadline and both ways to take part, which “here is how” only promised.

### W06 Give the point without a reveal

Put the point where the reader meets it first. A colon or a teaser sentence that holds it back for effect only makes the reader wait.

*Terms.* Setup and payoff: holding back the point so it lands as a reveal, often after a colon or a teaser sentence.

**Before** (An interactive version of our systems atlas, the guided story, part “Who holds the money and the market”)

> ~~This is where~~ the story starts~~:~~ who owns what.

**After**

> The story starts **with** who owns what.

What changed: The rewrite gives the answer in the first breath and runs two words shorter.

### W07 Stop when the facts stop

End a paragraph on its last useful fact, and cut any line after it that only restates the point.

*Terms.* Landing sentence: a closing line written to make a paragraph land with a flourish. Paragraph pinning: ending paragraph after paragraph on a short line that restates its point. Summary beat: a sentence that tells readers what they have read.

**Before** (An interactive version of our systems atlas, the guided story, part “Whether voters can fire them”)

> ~~Accountability depends on a real chance of losing.~~ In most districts the general election is easy to predict, so the contest that decides the seat is a low-turnout primary. Incumbents also help write the rules for their own races, from district maps to voting procedures. ~~When few officials face a competitive race, the pressure to serve the whole electorate fades.~~

**After**

> **The pressure on officials to serve the whole electorate depends on a real chance of losing.** In most districts the general election is easy to predict, so the contest that decides the seat is a low-turnout primary. Incumbents also help write the rules for their own races, from district maps to voting procedures.

What changed: The rewrite states the point once, at the start, so a listener hears the paragraph end on its last new fact.

### W08 Close without a slogan

End a piece or section on the last thing the reader needs, usually a step with its date. Where a sign-off line belongs, use the locked tagline.

*Terms.* Slogan-like closing sentence: a short, quotable line at the end of a piece that sounds like an advertisement and carries no fact.

**Before** (We wrote this article ending around the closing headline of our brand guide’s fifth edition, “Put the promise to work.”)

> The school board votes on the new bell schedule April 14. ~~Put the promise to work.~~ *Illustrative*

**After**

> The school board votes on the new bell schedule April 14. **Sign up with the board clerk by April 10 to speak before the vote.** *Illustrative*

What changed: The rewrite ends on a step and its date, so the last words a listener hears say what to do.

## 3. Say what is so

State what is true in plain sentences, and leave out the shapes writers use for effect.

### W09 Say what a thing is

Tell the reader what something is. Leave out the turn that first says what it is not, and the phrases set against each other for effect.

*Terms.* Corrective negation: saying what something is not before saying what it is, as in “X, not Y” or “not X but Y”. Antithesis: two opposed ideas set against each other in balanced phrases.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 10 Photography, headline)

> Photograph ~~the process, not the idea of it~~.

**After**

> Photograph **public work while it happens, such as a hearing or a polling place**.

What changed: The rewrite tells a photographer what to shoot, and a listener hears no “not” to undo.

### W10 Drop the matched pair

Write the relation between two things as a plain sentence. Two short halves set side by side sound like a slogan and leave the reader to supply the link.

*Terms.* Contrasting pair: two short matched phrases placed side by side for their rhythm. A hinge headline such as “The zoning code & the rent.” stays, since it joins a rule to its result under its own rules in chapter 04.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 13 In use)

> Written testimony counts~~. Spoken testimony counts more~~.

**After**

> Written testimony counts**, and speaking at the hearing carries more weight**.

What changed: The rewrite makes the comparison in one sentence, so a listener hears no echo of “counts” in the second half.

### W11 Skip the run of negatives

When you rule several things out, put them in one list under one verb and give the reason.

*Terms.* Negative parallelism: a run of matched negatives, such as “no this, no that”. Negative anaphora: starting clause after clause with the same negative word.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 04, the seal; the reason in the rewrite is the guide’s own, “Seals imply an authority we do not claim.”)

> ~~No crest, no shield, no founding date, no ring of words~~

**After**

> **Leave crests, shields, founding dates and rings of words off the seal, because they suggest an authority we do not claim.**

What changed: One verb carries the whole list in the rewrite, and a listener hears the reason with it.

### W12 List what the facts hold

Give a list as many items as the facts hold. When there are three, vary their shape so they do not fall into a chant.

*Terms.* Rule of three, or rhetorical triplet: grouping words or phrases in threes because three sounds finished. A plain list of three is fine when the facts have three parts.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 13 In use, the first sentence of a line on a workshop flyer)

> Ninety seconds: ~~the rule, the gap, the ask~~.

**After**

> You get 90 seconds. **Name the rule and where it falls short before you ask the committee for what you want**.

What changed: The rewrite turns the chant into one instruction a first-time speaker can follow, and read aloud it sounds like advice.

### W13 Give each sentence its own shape

Build each sentence in a paragraph on its own frame. When several in a row open the same way, combine them or recast one.

*Terms.* Parallel structure: sentences built on the same frame one after another, such as a run that each starts with “We”.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 02 Voice, the voice line)

> ~~We explain the rules. We expose the gaps. We connect people to action. We act.~~

**After**

> **We’ll explain the rules and track what happens to them. When a decision is coming, we’ll connect you with the people making it, so you can act in time.**

What changed: Four sentences on one frame become two of different lengths in the rewrite, the voice line we now use, which drops “We expose the gaps” and hands the last step to the reader on purpose. Our editorial commitment keeps its two-part shape, since locked lines keep their wording (H01).

## 4. Cut the padding

Take out the words that add weight and no meaning.

### W14 Pull stacked nouns apart

When three or more nouns pile up, pull them apart with a preposition or a verb, so a listener can find the main noun.

*Terms.* Stacked noun phrase: three or more nouns in a row, each describing the next, with the main noun last.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 07 the Signal Grid, figure caption)

> ~~Report cover kicker~~ on Prussian

**After**

> **The kicker, the short line above the title, on a report cover set** on Prussian**, our dark blue**

What changed: Read aloud, “report cover kicker” runs together, and the rewrite puts the main noun first and says where it sits.

### W15 Use the verb inside the noun

When a noun hides a verb, use the verb and name who does it.

*Terms.* Nominalization: a verb or adjective turned into a noun, such as “allocation” from “allocate” or “implementation” from “implement”.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, the sample research brief, page 07, source note)

> ~~An allocation~~ alone ~~establishes no claim about service quality~~.

**After**

> **How much a town sets aside for a service** does not, by itself, **show how good the service is**.

What changed: The rewrite brings back the verbs, so a listener hears who sets money aside and what the amount cannot show.

### W16 Drop the hedges and keep the grade

State a claim at the strength its evidence holds. When the evidence grade calls for “may” or “can”, use that word once and cut the other softeners.

*Terms.* Hedging qualifier: a word that softens a claim with no reason given, such as “perhaps” or “somewhat”. Grade verbs work differently: for a claim graded plausible, “may” or “can” reports the grade, as H08 requires.

**Before** (We wrote this line on our systems atlas’s profile of automatic voter registration, which grades the evidence for its effect on turnout as plausible.)

> Automatic voter registration ~~could perhaps help~~ turnout ~~somewhat in some places~~. *Illustrative*

**After**

> Automatic voter registration **may raise** turnout **a little. The atlas grades that evidence as plausible**. *Illustrative*

What changed: The rewrite lets one grade verb carry the doubt the evidence holds and names the grade, and read aloud it is one plain claim.

### W17 Cut the intensifiers

Cut words that try to add force, such as genuinely, really, truly, actually, simply and just. If the sentence feels weak without one, give it a stronger fact.

*Terms.* Filler intensifier: a word meant to add emphasis that adds no information.

**Before** (We wrote this line for the rule, since our published copy offered no clean case.)

> The hearing is ~~really~~ the last chance to speak before the council votes. *Illustrative*

**After**

> The hearing is the last chance to speak before the council votes **the same night**. *Illustrative*

What changed: The rewrite drops “really” and adds that the vote comes the same night, which gives a listener the urgency the word only signaled.

### W18 Use everyday verbs

Pick the word you would say out loud. Write “use” for leverage, and “show” for underscore or reflect.

*Terms.* Corporate register: the vocabulary of business memos. The atlas glossary’s “leverage point”, a systems term from Donella Meadows, is a defined term and stays.

**Before** (We wrote this line for the rule, since our published copy uses none of these verbs.)

> The county will ~~leverage~~ federal ~~funds~~ to ~~expand broadband access~~. *Illustrative*

**After**

> The county will **use** federal **money** to **bring broadband to more homes**. *Illustrative*

What changed: The rewrite uses the words a resident would say at a council meeting, and “more homes” tells a listener who gains.

### W19 Let the facts carry the feeling

Leave out exclamation points and cheering, and let the step itself carry the energy.

*Terms.* Performed enthusiasm: excitement acted out on the page in place of a reason to care.

**Before** (Our brand guide, fifth edition, chapter 02 Voice, which already listed it as a line to avoid)

> ~~Make your voice heard!~~

**After**

> **Before they vote, let the officials who decide know what you think.**

What changed: The rewrite names whom to tell and when, and read aloud it sounds like advice from a neighbor.

### W20 Size each word to its evidence

Give an adjective or a label no more weight than its evidence carries, and when we praise our own work, label the praise as a design judgment.

*Terms.* Inflated adjective: a word that claims more than its evidence, such as “stunning”, or a name wider than what was measured.

**Before** (An early draft of this guide, the first title of a turnout chart, November 6, 2026)

> Three of the four states with the most ~~eligible voters~~, Texas, Florida and New York, reported turnout below the national rate of 65.3 percent.

**After**

> Texas, Florida and New York, three of the four states with the most **citizens 18 and over**, reported turnout below the national 65.3 percent **by more than the survey’s margin of error**.

<small>Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey Voting and Registration Supplement, Table 4a, reported voting for states, November 2024, 50 states and DC. Retrieved November 6, 2026. Self-reported voting among citizens 18 and over; state margins of error reach 5.6 points.</small>

What changed: The survey counts citizens 18 and over, so the rewrite uses those words, and its margin-of-error test makes the title say only what a listener could check on the chart.

## 5. Marks and numbers

Set dashes, dates, numbers and signs the same way on every page.

### W21 Leave out the em dash

Where you want an em dash, end the sentence, or join the parts with a comma and a word such as “and” or “because”.

*Terms.* Em dash: the long dash, as wide as a capital M, that writers use to break into a sentence or tack a thought onto its end.

**Before** (We wrote this line for the rule, since our published copy holds no em dash to quote.)

> Public comment on the town budget closes Monday at 5 p.m. ~~—~~ the council votes the next night. *Illustrative*

**After**

> Public comment on the town budget closes Monday at 5 p.m.**, and** the council votes the next night. *Illustrative*

What changed: The rewrite’s comma and “and” tell a listener that the vote follows the deadline.

**H05 Write dates in words.** Write the month as a word, as in November 6, 2026. Numeric dates belong in file names and data.

> Retrieved November 6, 2026.

**H06 Spell out numbers under ten.** In prose, write one to nine as words. Charts, tables, measurements and data use figures.

> payrolls shrank in seven of the 57 months charted

<small>Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Employment Statistics, all nonfarm employees, monthly change, seasonally adjusted, January 2022 to September 2026, United States, via FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Retrieved November 6, 2026. August and September 2026 are preliminary and will be revised.</small>

**H07 Use the true minus sign.** Write a negative number with the minus sign (U+2212). It matches the plus sign’s width and lines up in tables, and a hyphen is too short to read as minus.

> The scale runs from −200,000 to +850,000.

<small>Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Employment Statistics, all nonfarm employees, monthly change, seasonally adjusted, January 2022 to September 2026, United States, via FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Retrieved November 6, 2026. August and September 2026 are preliminary and will be revised.</small>

## House rules for every page

**H01 Quote the locked lines exactly.** Quote the tagline, the mission, the reader promise and the editorial commitment word for word, and keep the pillars in their order: Explain, Track, Connect, Act. The locked lines keep their wording even where a writing rule above would shape a sentence differently.

> “A country that answers to its people.”

**H02 Explain a term the first time you use it.** Define each term of art in plain words where it first appears, using the atlas glossary’s wording when it fits. Keep the same term after that.

> Cloture, a Senate vote to end debate, takes 60 votes for most legislation.

<small>Our systems atlas, Who Do They Answer To?, glossary entry “Cloture”</small>

**H03 Label what is invented beside it.** Put the word Illustrative beside every invented person, quote, place or figure a reader could take for real.

> Alder County votes on backyard cottages March 9. Illustrative

**H04 Source every public policy statistic.** Give the publisher, the title or table, the observation period, the geography, the date retrieved and the qualification that matters, in that order.

> Source: U.S. Census Bureau, Current Population Survey Voting and Registration Supplement, Table 4a, reported voting for states, November 2024, 50 states and DC. Retrieved November 6, 2026. Self-reported voting among citizens 18 and over; state margins of error reach 5.6 points.

**H08 Let a title claim only what its evidence holds.** A title states only what its evidence shows, and its verb matches the evidence grade, as the table sets out. W16 shows the same verbs inside a sentence.

Before (An early draft of this guide, the first title of a jobs chart, November 6, 2026)

> The three-month average of monthly job ~~gains fell from 279,000 in January 2023 to~~ 51,000 in September 2026, and payrolls shrank in seven months.

After

> The three-month average of monthly job **change stood at** 51,000 in September 2026, **a preliminary figure,** and payrolls shrank in seven **of the 57** months **charted**.

<small>Source: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Current Employment Statistics, all nonfarm employees, monthly change, seasonally adjusted, January 2022 to September 2026, United States, via FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis. Retrieved November 6, 2026. August and September 2026 are preliminary and will be revised.</small>

| Grade | What it means | Title verb |
|---|---|---|
| Documented | An event or fact on the public record, such as a statute or an official statistic. | Record language, such as “passed” or “stood at”. |
| Well supported | Several credible studies, built to rule out other explanations, agree about direction. | A direct finding, such as “raised” or “lowered”. |
| Plausible | A coherent mechanism with some supporting evidence, from studies that are weak or few. | “Can” or “may”. |
| Contested | Credible studies disagree. | Both readings, at the same length. |
| Hypothesis | A proposed relationship that needs stronger evidence. | A label that marks it as untested. |

**H09 Keep a period whole.** No chart or sentence splits a period at an inauguration date, since a split invites credit or blame for one administration. Charts shown together share one time window and print the reason for it.

> Both jobs charts run from January 2022 to September 2026, so they hold three full years before the January 2025 inauguration and every month since.

**H10 Name parties in words.** Write a party’s name in words, in the same ink as the text around it. No election graphic, social cards included, codes a party with red or blue.

> Georgia: Democratic +0.2 in 2020, Republican +2.2 in 2024

<small>Source: Federal Election Commission, Federal Elections 2020 (Table 2) and Official 2024 Presidential General Election Results, compiled from state election offices, November 2020 and November 2024, 50 states and DC. Retrieved November 6, 2026. Margins are Republican minus Democratic share of all presidential votes; New York counts fused party lines.</small>

**H11 Say what an ID refers to.** *Proposed.* Name what an ID refers to the first time it appears, and print no bare code.

> Loop R10, the safe-seat loop

**H12 Use the neutral name for a contested policy.** *Proposed.* Call a contested policy by its official or plain name. When one side’s term has to appear, say who uses it.

> Public Law 119-21, the July 2025 tax law

## Before you publish

1. Could you say each sentence aloud to a neighbor, with the person who acts named first?
2. Do your sentences change in length and shape as the paragraph goes on?
3. Does the first sentence give the reader a fact or a step, and does the last one end on a fact?
4. Does each sentence say what something is, with no matched pair or “not this, but that” turn?
5. Have you turned every noun that hides a verb back into its verb, and pulled apart every stack of nouns?
6. Does each claim sit at the strength its evidence grade allows, with no word added for force or caution?
7. Are the locked lines exact, and does every invented line, person, place or figure carry the Illustrative label?
8. Does every public policy statistic carry its full source line?
9. Are parties named in words, in the same ink as the text, and is each contested policy called by its neutral name?
10. Are months written as words, numbers under ten spelled out, minus signs true and em dashes gone?
