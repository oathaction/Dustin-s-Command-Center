### F1. When FOIA's 20 working days start (ST-M2; for the systems designer)

- **The law:** "The 20-day period under clause (i) shall commence on the date on which the request is first received by the appropriate component of the agency, but in any event not later than ten days after the request is first received by any component of the agency that is designated in the agency's regulations under this section to receive requests under this section." Clause (i) sets the period: "determine within 20 days (excepting Saturdays, Sundays, and legal public holidays) after the receipt of any such request". The same paragraph allows two pauses only: the agency "may make one request to the requester for information and toll the 20-day period while it is awaiting such information", or pause "if necessary to clarify with the requester issues regarding fee assessment".
- **Where:** 5 U.S.C. 552(a)(6)(A). The start rule is the unnumbered paragraph that follows clause (ii) inside subparagraph (A); the reviewer's "552(a)(6)(A)(ii)" points to that paragraph, and "552(a)(6)(A), the paragraph after clause (ii)" is the exact place. United States Code, 2024 edition, govinfo, https://www.govinfo.gov/link/uscode/5/552?type=usc&year=2024&link-type=html . Read November 16, 2026. The sidecar `govinfo-foia-request-steps.meta.json` already cites "(a)(6)(A)" in its `table_id` and records this section current through Public Law 119-138 (its note of November 13).
- **So:** the clock starts when the right office receives the request, or 10 days after any designated office receives it, whichever comes first (the law says "ten days" here and does not say working days). An acknowledgment starts nothing. The reviewer's words hold: "within 20 working days, counted from the day the right office receives the request, and no later than 10 days after any office does". If the plate needs the full rule, "any office the agency names to receive requests" is the law's limit on "any office".

### F2. California's signature sampling rule (ST-S3; for the tracker designer)

- **The rule, in order:** (1) a raw count; if it "lacks 100 percent of the signatures required", the measure fails and "no further action will be taken on that petition" (Elec. Code 9030(b), (c)); (2) a random sample, at least 500 signatures or 3 percent of those filed in each county, whichever is greater (9030(d)); (3) the Secretary of State projects the statewide total of valid signatures; under 95 percent of the number required, "the proposed initiative measure fails to qualify for the ballot" (9030(f)); over 110 percent, it is eligible (9030(g), 9033); between 95 and 110 percent, county officials "verify every signature on the petition", a "full check" (9031(a)), which adds up to 60 business days, after which it qualifies or fails on the full count (9031).
- **Where:** California Secretary of State, Statewide Initiative Guide, revised January 2026, Chapter II, Verification of Signatures (pages 14 and 15), and "Overview of Qualification Methods", https://elections.cdn.sos.ca.gov/ballot-measures/pdf/statewide-initiative-guide.pdf . Read November 16, 2026. The Elections Code itself (leginfo.legislature.ca.gov) refused again today (403), so the sections are cited as the guide gives them, as on November 14.
- **So:** a sample that projects fewer valid signatures than required stops a measure only when the projection is under 95 percent. The reviewer's words are right: "On July 14, a random sample of the signatures projected fewer than 95 percent of the valid signatures the law requires, so no full count followed."

### F3. The majority a bill needs, against full membership (ST-S6, CE-S1; for the systems designer)

- **A majority of those voting, with a quorum present.** The Constitution: "a Majority of each shall constitute a Quorum to do Business" (Article I, Section 5) and "The Vice President of the United States shall be President of the Senate, but shall have no Vote, unless they be equally divided" (Article I, Section 3); National Archives transcription, https://www.archives.gov/founding-docs/constitution-transcript . House: "Most business that comes before the House is decided by a majority vote", and "A two-thirds vote means two-thirds of those voting, a quorum being present, and not two-thirds of the entire membership" (House Practice, 2017 edition, chapter 58, Voting, section 28, govinfo, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-HPRACTICE-115/pdf/GPO-HPRACTICE-115-59.pdf ). "In case of a tie vote, a question shall be lost" (Rules of the House, 119th Congress, Clerk's printing of January 16, 2025, Rule XX, clause 1(c), https://clerk.house.gov/legislative/house-rules.pdf ). Senate: "In most cases a simple majority is required for a measure to pass. In the case of a tie, the vice president may cast the tie-breaking vote." (U.S. Senate, "About Voting", https://www.senate.gov/about/powers-procedures/voting.htm ); "A tie vote on a question defeats it" (Riddick's Senate Procedure, 1992, Voting, govinfo, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/GPO-RIDDICK-1992/pdf/GPO-RIDDICK-1992-143.pdf ). All read November 16, 2026.
- **Where "218 of 435" comes from:** the House's own page, "If the bill passes by simple majority (218 of 435)" (https://www.house.gov/the-house-explained/the-legislative-process , read again today, same words). It holds only when all 435 seats are filled and every member votes yes or no.

### F4. When a conference committee meets (CE-S2; for the systems designer)

- **Three ways to one text.** "When a bill has been agreed to in identical form by both bodies, either: (1) without amendment by the second House to consider it; (2) by the first House's concurrence in the second House's amendments; or (3) by agreement in both bodies to the conference report, a copy of the bill is enrolled for presentation to the President." And: "The mere fact that each House may have separately passed its own bill on a subject is not sufficient to make either bill eligible for conference. One House must first take the additional step of amending and then passing the bill of the other House to form the basis for a conference." A chamber may also "further amend" the other's amendments. Source: "How Our Laws Are Made", House Document 110-49 (2007), parts XV, "Final Action on Amended Bill", "Request for a Conference", XVI and XVII, "Enrollment", govinfo, https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/CDOC-110hdoc49/pdf/CDOC-110hdoc49.pdf . Read November 16, 2026 (the dashes in the first quote are the document's, set here as commas).
- **So:** a conference committee meets only after one chamber has amended the other's bill, the first chamber disagrees, and both agree to a conference. The House's page ("Finally, a conference committee ... works out any differences") is the source of the plate's present words and overstates it. The reviewer's words are sound: "Where the chambers passed different texts, they settle the differences by amending each other's text or in a conference committee, and both pass the same text." How often each way is used was not counted (Not tested).

### F9. What a governor does after a Legislature passes an initiative (CE-S17; for the tracker designer and the editor)

- **Ohio: the governor acts, as on any bill.** Article II, Section 1b sets the referendum clock from the day "the law as passed by the general assembly shall have been filed by the governor in the office of the secretary of state", and Section 16 says "If the governor approves an act, he shall sign it, it becomes law and he shall file it with the secretary of state", with a veto that three-fifths of each house can override. The only bar on a veto is for a law "proposed by initiative petition and approved by the electors". Ohio Constitution, Article II, Sections 1b and 16, https://codes.ohio.gov/ohio-constitution/section-2.1b and /section-2.16 , read November 16, 2026.
- **Michigan: no governor.** "No law initiated or adopted by the people shall be subject to the veto power of the governor" (Article II, Section 9, https://www.legislature.mi.gov/Laws/MCL?objectName=mcl-Article-II-9 ). The House Fiscal Agency's analysis of Senate Bill 8 of 2025 (enacted as Public Act 1 of 2025): "If the legislature enacts it, the law proposed by the petition becomes a public act without going to the governor for approval. (The governor cannot veto such an enactment.)" (https://legislature.mi.gov/documents/2025-2026/billanalysis/House/pdf/2025-HLA-0008-YDR1ZHT0.pdf ). The Legislative Service Bureau's 2018 Public Acts Table lists Public Acts 337 and 338 of 2018, "Initiated Law; hours and wages" and "Initiated Law; benefits; fair employment practices", with no date under "Governor Approved" and filed September 5 (https://www.legislature.mi.gov/documents/2017-2018/publicacttable/pdf/2018-PAT.pdf ). All read November 16, 2026.
- **Massachusetts: the governor acts, by the constitution's text; no official page I found says so in words.** "No bill or resolve of the senate or house of representatives shall become a law, and have force as such, until it shall have been laid before the governor for his revisal" (Part the Second, Chapter I, Section I, Article II). Article XLVIII makes no exception for a law the General Court enacts on an initiative petition, and its General Provisions put the General Court's changes to a law the people approved "Subject to the veto power of the governor". Massachusetts Constitution, https://malegislature.gov/Laws/Constitution , read November 16, 2026. That a law the General Court enacts this way goes to the governor is my reading of those texts. The Elections Division's guide (May 2025) and the Supreme Judicial Court's opinion of April 27, 2026 (Senate No. 3066) were read and do not address it (Claims I could not verify).
- **So:** "Passed by the Legislature" is not yet law in Ohio, and by the text not in Massachusetts, until the governor signs or the Legislature overrides a veto; in Michigan it is law once enacted, with no governor. Suggested words for Rule 11.10: "Passed by the Legislature on May 2. In Massachusetts and Ohio it then goes to the governor, as a bill does; in Michigan it becomes law without one." I read no other state, so the words should not say "most states". Each state's own rule for when such a law takes effect was not read today.
