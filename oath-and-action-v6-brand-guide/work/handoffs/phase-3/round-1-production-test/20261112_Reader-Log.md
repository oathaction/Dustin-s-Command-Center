# Production test 2, round 1: a reader new to the subject

Simulated panel, untested with real people (DR-202). One simulated reader, run by the UX and accessibility lead on November 12, 2026. The reader is an adult who follows the news, has never read a statistics text and has not read chapters 1 to 13. They read three chapter 14 entries and two chapter 15 plates in the strict build, each with its rule, "Use it when", "Why it works" and its specimen's evidence label, title, measure line, reading guide and notes. For each: what it taught, what confused them, and the word that stopped them. The words quoted come from the entry files, the specs and chapter 15's file.

## Rule 14.20, slope chart (S20, homeownership by age)

- **Taught.** A line from left to right shows whether each group went up or down between two years, and lines that cross mean the order changed. The chart picked its years for a reason and says so; the second note shows why 2015 makes the decade look like a gain.
- **Confused.** "Passes its margin of error of about 0.8 points at 90 percent confidence": the reader understood that some changes count and others do not, but not what a margin of error is. The entry cites Rule 14.10 but explains neither term; the gloss lives in chapter 13.
- **The word that stopped them.** "Householders". The measure line says "by age of the householder" without saying who that is in a home.

## Rule 14.37, a line with a gap (S31, unemployment rate)

- **Taught.** Never draw a line across a month with no figure; the gray column and its words show where the record stops. Note 3 explains "seasonally adjusted" in one sentence, and the reader liked it.
- **Confused.** The grade line, "Documented: a Bureau of Labor Statistics survey of households, as copied by FRED": two sources in one line. The source line at the foot names FRED in full ("via FRED, Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis"), but the reader meets the bare name first, at the top.
- **The word that stopped them.** "FRED".

## Rule 14.129, heat table (S37, feasibility of reform routes)

- **Taught.** Darker cells mean more feasible, every cell also prints its word, and the cells are not added up.
- **Confused.** The label says "Estimate", but the grade line calls it "the atlas's own judgment"; the reader expected an estimate to be a number. The title's "Of 38 routes through an act of Congress" read at first as 38 acts. Blank cells in the table were not explained where the table is: the reason sits in the measure line above the drawing ("Each reform shows only the routes the atlas names for it").
- **The words that stopped them.** "Article V", a column head with no gloss, and "routes".

## Rule 15.3 plates, signed links

- **Taught.** A plus means two things move the same way and a minus means opposite ways; a plus is not good news. The failure plate made the point quickly: a key that calls plus "positive effect" turns more hostile content into good news.
- **Confused.** "With the model's other conditions held steady": the reader did not know what model or which conditions.
- **The word that stopped them.** "Variables", used as if known; chapter 15's opener and Rule 15.2 define it, but this reader arrived at Rule 15.3 from a link.

## Rule 15.13 plate, Loop R1 and its weakest step

- **Taught.** A loop is only as strong as its weakest step; the dashed line marks it, and the label gives the whole loop that step's grade. The step list beside it gives each grade word with its gloss ("Plausible, some evidence"), which the reader found the clearest part of all five.
- **Confused.** Each step reads "money, raises". The reader took "money" for an amount; it names the kind of connection, and nothing on the plate says so. The three short bars across two lines were not explained on the plate.
- **The word that stopped them.** "Money", as a label on a line.

## Patterns across the five

1. Terms defined in chapter 13 or earlier in chapter 15 are used bare in an entry or a plate a reader may reach by a link: margin of error, variables, the line code's ticks. A one-clause gloss at first use on each plate would carry them.
2. Proper names of sources and of the Constitution's parts appear before their gloss or without one: FRED at the top of S31 (named in full only in its source line), Article V.
3. Each title, read alone, told the reader the finding. The reading guides (S31's especially) and the plain-words notes worked best.
