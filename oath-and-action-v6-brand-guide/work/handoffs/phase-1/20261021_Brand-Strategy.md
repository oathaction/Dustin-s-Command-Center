## 1. Positioning and the brand idea

**Positioning.** For people in every state who live under public rules they rarely get to read, Oath & Action is a nonpartisan publication and set of tools. Its reader promise: "We explain how a decision is made and give you a practical way to take part." Readers can check it: claims carry a source and an evidence grade, and every explanation ends with a step.

**Points of difference**
- It explains the mechanism, the path from a rule to its result, with evidence graded the way the atlas grades it.
- Every piece turns at the hinge divider to "What you can do."
- It serves readers of every party and state with the same record and the same way in.
- It labels what it argues. A position piece carries a label before the headline and a summary of the strongest case against it.

**The hinge.** Dustin's idea (DC-05): the ampersand is a promise or commitment, then action. The locked lines already follow it.

| Locked line, exact | The commitment | The action |
|---|---|---|
| Mission: "Help people understand and improve the rules that shape their lives." | understand | improve |
| Reader promise: "We explain how a decision is made and give you a practical way to take part." | explain how a decision is made | give you a practical way to take part |
| Editorial commitment: "Clarity is our duty. Action is our promise." | clarity, our duty | action, our promise |
| Pillars: Explain → Track → Connect → Act | Explain, Track | Connect, Act |
| Tagline: "A country that answers to its people." | names the result when the loop closes | |

The pillar order stays exactly as locked. The hinge sits between Track and Connect, where the reader stops learning about a decision and starts taking part in it.

**What the ampersand joins.** DC-05 names pairs at the level of ideas. A hinge headline must name a rule, so each idea reaches the page through a concrete pair. The editor tested these (`20261021_Voice-Line-And-Headlines.md`, section 4).

| Idea the ampersand stands for | How it reaches a headline |
|---|---|
| A commitment, then action | "The campaign pledge & the roll call." |
| Cause and effect | "The zoning code & the rent." |
| Problem and solution | "The permit delay & the by-right rule." (studio draft; C15 asks the editor to redraft "The proposal & the outcome.", which names no problem) |
| You and me | "Your signature & the ballot question." |
| Common ground across the left and the right | "The compromise & the law." |

Every hinge headline sets the V5 master ampersand (`work/extract/v5-polished/sprite/amp.svg`) at the words' cap height, never the display face's own ampersand. Both halves of a pair take the same color and weight. Only the ampersand is red, as the view's working red (Rule 01.1).

**House format for "you & me" pieces.** Arguments for and against, then each party's majorities, sourced. The Program for Public Consultation works this way: respondents rate the strongest arguments on each side, and results show Republican and Democratic majorities side by side (research ledger I4, checked through search results only; its panels are online opt-in).

**The hinge diagram as a loop.** Dustin's mockups circle the ampersand with two dashed arcs. Each arc carries arrowheads at both ends, so the figure reads as an exchange between two sides. When both arcs run one way, it becomes a loop: the promise becomes action along the top, and the result informs the next promise along the bottom. That is the definition of a feedback loop in Atlas V7's glossary: "A chain of causes that returns to its starting variable."

Two atlas loops carry the brand's argument:
- Loop R9, "Taking part in a successful repair may support the next one." V7 grades the full loop a hypothesis: "The complete loop is a design hypothesis." Its chain is effective participation, accountable delivery and warranted trust.
- Loop B4, "Electoral consequences can improve public performance." V7 calls it "The main balancing loop in the model," well-supported where seats are competitive. It describes the condition the tagline names.

So the brand presents its loop as a goal and a design, and any claim about its effect carries Loop R9's grade and the verb "can" or "may." The drawing is the identity board's V2 hinge diagram (`work/boards/phase-1/identity/07-hinge/20261021_Hinge-Display-Master_V2.svg` and the compact master beside it), which the board inlines:
- Arrowheads point one way, clockwise, reading left to right along the top.
- Arcs are solid hairlines. Atlas V7 draws hypotheses dotted and plausible links dashed, so no brand graphic uses a dotted or dashed line (D1-21).
- When Loop R9 appears inside an atlas graphic, it follows the systems board's line code: its conceptual steps take the hollow line, two hairlines around an empty core, with V7's grade word, "Hypothesis."
- The labels under the words stay V5's, UNDERSTAND and IMPACT. The display master holds them at 13 px from 526 px wide; below that the compact master drops them.

**Brand narrative** (232 words)

> Every public rule starts with people who promised to serve: a council that adopts a budget, a legislature that passes a bill. Then the rule reaches a household as a rent renewal, a pay stub, a ballot or a power bill.
>
> Oath & Action follows that path for readers in every state. We read the rule and show who holds the decision. The record stays within reach while the decision moves. When it is time to act, we point to the office that answers for it and the step a reader can take.
>
> The ampersand in our name is the hinge between a promise and the action that keeps it. It joins a rule to its result and a problem to the fixes on the table. It also joins people: you and a neighbor who lives under the same rule and may vote another way. Each of you gets the record and a way to take part.
>
> The hinge turns in a loop. People who take part and see the result can hold the next promise to account, and the people who made it answer to them. Our tagline names that goal: "A country that answers to its people."
>
> We are nonpartisan. When credible sources disagree, we show both readings and the evidence for each. When we take a position, we label it before the headline and summarize the strongest case against it.

## 3. Brand architecture

**Model.** One masterbrand. Oath & Action names the publication, signs every product and holds the only ampersand in any product name.

| Product | Name rule | Explain | Track | Connect | Act |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| The publication (site) | Oath & Action | Leads | Supports | Supports | Supports |
| Newsletter | Oath & Action, "the weekly briefing" as descriptor; issues numbered and dated | Leads | Supports | Supports | Ends every issue |
| Atlas | Who Do They Answer To? The Oath & Action Systems Atlas | Pathways | Register | Who can act | Reforms as options studied |
| Trackers | {Subject} Tracker, with an "as of" date on every view | | Leads | | Links to Act |
| Reports | Oath & Action Report {number}: {title}, dated | Leads | Supports | Supports | Closing page |
| Explainers | Series name, slash, topic: "Reform 101 / Ranked-choice voting" | Leads | | | Hinge divider |
| Act pieces | Kicker "Act" plus the kind: "How to take part" or "Our position" | | | Supports | Leads |

**Naming rules**
1. Product names describe what the product does. The atlas is the only product titled as a question.
2. No product name contains an ampersand. V5's rule of one ampersand moment per composition stays, and a partner's ampersand is written as "and."
3. Every piece carries a content label: Explainer, Tracker, Analysis, Report, Act: How to take part, or Act: Our position.
4. Keep V5's series names, with one change: "Dispatch from the Front" becomes "Dispatch," which V5's own kicker already uses ("Dispatch / H.4212"). A front is a war metaphor.
5. Drop "scorecards" from Track. Scorecards that grade individual officials are an advocacy format. Trackers follow the rule and the record.
6. The atlas, the trackers and the data products never take a position. A reform in the atlas is an option studied, shown with its evidence grade. Positions appear only in labeled Act pieces.

## 5. The Act pattern (DC-01)

The pattern lives in 13 Evidence and sourcing.

| | Process piece | Position piece |
|---|---|---|
| Kicker | "Act · How to take part" in plain type | "Our position" as a filled Charcoal label, first in the kicker, before the headline |
| Ask | A step with a date and a place, with no side taken | Support or oppose a named measure |
| Both readings | Supporters' and opponents' arguments, equal length, sourced | "The strongest case against," before the ask |
| Before the ask | Hinge divider, its ampersand Charcoal | Hinge divider, its ampersand Charcoal, then the disclosure line directly above the button |
| Red | The ask button, the view's one working red (Rule 01.1) | The ask button, the view's one working red. The label stays Charcoal |
| Links | The tracker for the measure | The neutral explainer and tracker, the methods page and the position log |

**What keeps a position piece fair**
1. The label comes first, before the headline, and the disclosure repeats above the ask. A shared card keeps the label in its top-left corner.
2. "The strongest case against" summarizes the opposing case as its supporters would put it, with sources, at no less than a third of the argument's length.
3. Every claim keeps its evidence grade, and the verb matches it (Master Brief section 15).
4. The piece argues for a measure and describes its opponents by office or role, never by party as a villain.
5. "What we don't know" names the open questions.
6. Process facts (who decides, when, how) match the neutral tracker word for word.
7. The disclosure names who decided the position, the date and any funder interest. A public position log lists every position, so readers can check the record across issues.
8. Positions never appear in the atlas, trackers or data products.

The board shows both kinds as mock layouts, with "Illustrative" beside every invented name, date, figure and source.

## 6. Nonpartisan rule set

The set lives in 01 Purpose as Rules 01.1 to 01.20, one public set that other chapters cite. The IDs follow the architecture map's Rule NN.n form, which cannot collide with Atlas V7 IDs (C4). Rule 01.1 is the red rule, quoted from the creative director review; it replaces the earlier red and Prussian wording.

**Color**
- Rule 01.1. The wordmark's ampersand and the lockmark's lever are signature red and do not count. Each view (one screen, card, slide or printed page) then carries one working red, in this order of precedence: an error the reader must fix; the step a reader can take now; the one point the headline names, which in a systems figure is where a reform acts. A hinge headline's ampersand uses the view's working red. The hinge divider's ampersand is Charcoal. The Act handle is red only when the Act mark is itself the call to act, as on a social tile. Red never sits on Prussian.
- Rule 01.2. A comparison of two sides never uses red against any blue, or red against Verdigris. Two sides take Charcoal and Stone, or solid and outline, with direct labels.
- Rule 01.3. Diverging scales center on Newsprint, use two hues outside the red and blue pair, and name both directions in words. The data visualization lead sets values in Phase 3.
- Rule 01.4. Identity marks on Prussian use treatment D, one color White, as V5 sets its letters. Data graphics on Prussian keep the token table's Newsprint (C2).
- Rule 01.5. Maps never color places by party or election result. A map of a rule uses the neutral palette and pattern, with a key beside it.

**Imagery**
- Rule 01.6. Domes, columns and flags appear only in a captioned documentary photograph of that place or event, and never as decoration, icon or texture.
- Rule 01.7. Brand graphics carry no party symbols, candidate photos, campaign merchandise or legible protest slogans. A party logo can appear as evidence inside a sourced document.
- Rule 01.8. Photographs show people of different ages, places and roles doing public work, officials included. Captions give who, what, where and when.

**Rotation**
- Rule 01.9. Within a chapter, no V7 domain supplies more than one example in four, and no state more than one in five. Massachusetts examples may appear (DC-06).
- Rule 01.10. Examples rotate across federal, state, county or city and school district decisions, and across regions.
- Rule 01.11. When a mechanism is used by majorities of either party, show cases from both. Loop R4a, "Majorities draw maps that help them keep control," is the test case.
- Rule 01.12. A rotation log records each example's domain, state, level and the coalition that usually backs any reform shown. The studio reviews it at each gate.

**Language**
- Rule 01.13. Name the office that decides. Name a party only when party is part of the mechanism, such as a party-line vote, with the count and source.
- Rule 01.14. No grievance framing: "the rule you are angry about," "rigged," "broken," "crisis." Ask questions a supporter of the current rule could ask too.
- Rule 01.15. No war or sports metaphors such as fight, battle or front.
- Rule 01.16. Contested claims show both readings at the same length, with the same verbs and type.
- Rule 01.17. The fairness test: a reader who supports the current rule would accept its description.

**Hinge pairs**
- Rule 01.18. Both halves of a hinge pair share one color and weight. Only the ampersand is red.
- Rule 01.19. People pairs join people through a shared rule or place. The order of people in pairs rotates across pieces.
- Rule 01.20. "Left," "right" or party names enter a hinge headline only to report a sourced point of agreement, such as a bill with sponsors from both parties.
