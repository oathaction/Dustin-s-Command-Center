In Congress

  1. 1

    Introduced and sent to committee

    A member sponsors the bill in the House or the Senate, and a committee studies it.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, retrieved November 11, 2026

  2. 2

    Passed by the first chamber

    The first chamber passes it by a majority of the members who vote, 218 when all 435 House members vote or 51 when all 100 senators do. A tie fails, and in the Senate the Vice President may break it.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, retrieved November 11, 2026; U.S. Constitution, Article I, Sections 3 and 5, retrieved November 16, 2026; House Rule XX, clause 1, as printed January 16, 2025, retrieved November 16, 2026; U.S. Senate, “About Voting”, retrieved November 16, 2026

  3. 3

    In the House, in place of the usual floor rules

    Suspension of the rules

    The House takes the bill up on a Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday, debates it for no more than 40 minutes and passes it with two-thirds of the members voting.

    Then stage 6, or stage 7 if the House votes second.

    Source: House Rule XV, clause 1, as printed January 16, 2025, retrieved November 14, 2026

  4. 4

    In the Senate, for most bills

    Cloture

    Three-fifths of the Senate, 60 of 100, vote to end debate before the vote on the bill. Short of 60, the debate goes on and the bill waits.

    With 60, on to the Senate’s vote, stage 2 or stage 6.

    Source: U.S. Senate, “About Filibusters and Cloture”, retrieved November 11, 2026

  5. 5

    In the Senate, for a budget reconciliation bill

    Reconciliation

    Reconciliation is a budget procedure that lets qualifying tax and spending bills pass the Senate by a simple majority. The law holds debate to 20 hours, so the Senate needs no cloture vote. A senator can object to a part that does not change spending or revenue, and if the presiding officer agrees, that part comes out of the bill.

    Then stage 6, or stage 7 if the Senate votes second.

    Source: 2 U.S.C. 641 and 644, as in force on November 14, 2026, the day we retrieved them

  6. 6

    Passed by the second chamber

    The second chamber passes it by the same count as stage 2, or by one of the paths at stages 3 to 5.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, retrieved November 11, 2026

  7. 7

    One text for both chambers

    The two chambers pass the same text. Where their versions differ, they settle the differences by amending each other’s text or in a conference committee.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, retrieved November 11, 2026; U.S. House, “How Our Laws Are Made”, House Document 110-49, retrieved November 16, 2026

  8. At the President

  9. 8

    Sent to the President

    The Government Publishing Office prints the final text, a step called enrolling, and Congress presents it to the President, who has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to act.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, retrieved November 11, 2026; U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, clause 2, retrieved November 11, 2026

  10. 9

    If the President signs it

    Signed

    The President signs it, and it becomes law.

    Then stage 13.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, retrieved November 11, 2026

  11. 10

    If the President vetoes it

    Veto and override

    The President returns it with objections. Two-thirds of each chamber can still make it law.

    Short of two-thirds in either chamber, the bill dies.

    With two-thirds in each, then stage 13.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, retrieved November 11, 2026

  12. 11

    If the President neither signs nor vetoes it

    Law without a signature

    The President lets 10 days pass, Sundays excepted, while Congress is in session, and the bill becomes law.

    Then stage 13.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, retrieved November 11, 2026

  13. 12

    If Congress adjourns first

    Pocket veto

    Congress adjourns within those 10 days, and the unsigned bill does not become law.

    The bill dies.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, retrieved November 11, 2026

  14. 13

    Public law

    The Office of the Federal Register gives it a number and prints it in the Statutes at Large.

    Source: National Archives, “Public Laws”, retrieved November 11, 2026

  15. Once it is law

  16. 14

    Funded by appropriation

    Congress funds the law in an appropriation, since money leaves the Treasury only through one made by law. If the President proposes to cancel some of that money, the President must still release it for spending unless Congress passes a rescission bill, which cancels it, within the 45-day period the law sets.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 9, retrieved November 11, 2026; 2 U.S.C. 683, as in force on November 14, 2026, the day we retrieved it

  17. 15

    Put to work by agency rules

    The agency publishes a proposed rule, takes comments and publishes the final rule at least 30 days before it takes effect, unless the law makes an exception.

    Source: 5 U.S.C. 553, as in force on November 11, 2026, the day we retrieved it

  18. 16

    Reported to Congress

    The agency reports each rule to Congress, and a major rule waits at least 60 days to take effect. Congress can end a rule with a joint resolution of disapproval, passed and signed like a bill.

    The rule ends, and may not return in substantially the same form.

    Source: 5 U.S.C. 801(a), as in force on November 11, 2026, the day we retrieved it; 5 U.S.C. 801(b), as in force on November 12, 2026, the day we retrieved it

  19. 17

    Taken to court

    A person harmed can sue in a district court and appeal. A court can hold an agency’s rule unlawful and set it aside.

    The rule, or the part the court names, is set aside.

    Source: U.S. Courts, “Court Role and Structure”, retrieved November 14, 2026; 5 U.S.C. 706, as in force on November 12, 2026, the day we retrieved it

  20. Undoing it

  21. 18

    Ended by a sunset

    A date in the law’s own text ends a part of it, unless a later act moves the date.

    That part ends on its date.

    Source: Public Law 115-97, section 11022, approved December 22, 2017, retrieved November 11, 2026

  22. 19

    Struck down by a court

    A court can rule that a law, or a part of it, violates the Constitution, a power the Supreme Court set out in Marbury v. Madison in 1803. The Supreme Court’s most recent decision to hold part of an Act of Congress unconstitutional, in its opinions through November 9, 2026, came on June 30, 2026, when it held that the limits on what a political party may spend in coordination with its candidates, in 52 U.S.C. 30116(d), violate the First Amendment.

    The part the court holds void no longer applies.

    Source: Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803), retrieved November 14, 2026; National Republican Senatorial Committee v. Federal Election Commission, No. 24-621, retrieved November 14, 2026

  23. 20

    Repealed or changed

    Congress passes a later act that repeals or rewrites it.

    A repeal is a new act: back to stage 1.

    Source: Public Law 119-21, section 70104, approved July 4, 2025, retrieved November 11, 2026
