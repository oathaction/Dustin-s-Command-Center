Route FS“Federal statute”

Our systems atlas, as of October 15, 2026: Act of Congress, including appropriations and reconciliation. Who acts: House, Senate, President.

Documentedon the public record: the law or rule behind each stage, with the date we read it

What the marks mean

  • A stage every enacted bill passes, in order, on the dark line
  • A path a bill can take instead, set to one side on a short arrow, saying where it leaves and where it goes next
  • What can follow the law, on the lighter line
  • A path that stops there
  • The line on the left: a repeal is a new act, so it runs back to stage 1

In Congress

  1. 1

    Introduced and sent to committee

    A member sponsors the bill in the House or the Senate, and a committee studies it.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, read November 11, 2026

  2. 2

    Passed by the first chamber

    A simple majority: 218 of 435 in the House, or 51 of 100 in the Senate.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, read November 11, 2026

  3. 3

    In the House, in place of the usual floor rules

    Suspension of the rules

    Forty minutes of debate, and two-thirds of the members voting, on a Monday, Tuesday or Wednesday.

    Then stage 6, or stage 7 if the House votes second.

    Source: House Rule XV, clause 1, read November 14, 2026

  4. 4

    In the Senate, for most bills

    Cloture

    Three-fifths of the Senate, 60 of 100, vote to end debate before the vote on the bill. Short of 60, the debate goes on and the bill waits.

    With 60, on to the Senate’s vote, stage 2 or stage 6.

    Source: U.S. Senate, “About Filibusters and Cloture”, read November 11, 2026

  5. 5

    In the Senate, for a budget reconciliation bill

    Reconciliation

    Debate is held to 20 hours, so no cloture vote is needed and a simple majority passes it. A part that does not change spending or revenue is struck when a senator raises a point of order and the chair agrees.

    Then stage 6, or stage 7 if the Senate votes second.

    Source: 2 U.S.C. 641, read November 14, 2026; 2 U.S.C. 644, read November 14, 2026

  6. 6

    Passed by the second chamber

    A simple majority, by the same paths as stage 2.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, read November 11, 2026

  7. 7

    One text for both chambers

    A conference committee settles the differences, and both chambers approve the same text.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, read November 11, 2026

  8. At the President

  9. 8

    Enrolled and presented

    The final text is printed and sent to the President, who has 10 days, Sundays excepted, to act.

    Source: U.S. House, “The Legislative Process”, read November 11, 2026

  10. 9

    Signed

    The President signs it, and it becomes law.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, read November 11, 2026

  11. 10

    If the President vetoes it

    Veto and override

    The President returns it with objections. Two-thirds of each chamber can still make it law.

    Short of two-thirds in either chamber, the bill dies.

    With two-thirds in each, then stage 13.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, read November 11, 2026

  12. 11

    If the President neither signs nor vetoes it

    Law without a signature

    Ten days pass, Sundays excepted, while Congress sits, and it becomes law.

    Then stage 13.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, read November 11, 2026

  13. 12

    If Congress adjourns first

    Pocket veto

    Congress adjourns within those 10 days, and the unsigned bill does not become law.

    The bill dies.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 7, read November 11, 2026

  14. 13

    Public law

    The Office of the Federal Register gives it a number and prints it in the Statutes at Large.

    Source: National Archives, “Public Laws”, read November 11, 2026

  15. Once it is law

  16. 14

    Funded by appropriation

    Money leaves the Treasury only through an appropriation made by law. If the President proposes to cancel some, the money must be released for spending unless Congress passes a rescission bill within the 45-day period the law sets.

    Source: U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 9, read November 11, 2026; 2 U.S.C. 683, read November 14, 2026

  17. 15

    Put to work by agency rules

    The agency publishes a proposed rule, takes comments and publishes the final rule at least 30 days before it takes effect, unless the law makes an exception.

    Source: 5 U.S.C. 553, read November 11, 2026

  18. 16

    Reported to Congress

    A major rule waits at least 60 days. A joint resolution of disapproval, passed and signed like a bill, ends it.

    The rule ends, and may not return in substantially the same form.

    Source: 5 U.S.C. 801 and 802, read November 11, 2026

  19. 17

    Taken to court

    A person harmed can sue in a district court and appeal. A court can hold an agency’s rule unlawful and set it aside.

    The rule, or the part the court names, is set aside.

    Source: U.S. Courts, “Court Role and Structure”, read November 14, 2026; 5 U.S.C. 706, read November 12, 2026

  20. Undoing it

  21. 18

    Ended by a sunset

    A date in the law’s own text ends a part of it, unless a later act moves the date.

    That part ends on its date.

    Source: Public Law 115-97, section 11022, read November 11, 2026

  22. 19

    Struck down by a court

    A court can rule that a law, or a part of it, violates the Constitution. In the Supreme Court’s words of 1803, “a law repugnant to the constitution is void”. The latest such ruling we found came on June 30, 2026, when the Supreme Court held that the limits on what a political party may spend in coordination with its candidates, in 52 U.S.C. 30116(d), violate the First Amendment.

    The part the court holds void no longer applies.

    Source: Marbury v. Madison, 5 U.S. 137 (1803), read November 14, 2026; National Republican Senatorial Committee v. Federal Election Commission, No. 24-621, read November 14, 2026

  23. 20

    Repealed or changed

    Congress passes a later act that repeals or rewrites it.

    A repeal is a new act: back to stage 1.

    Source: Public Law 119-21, section 70104, read November 11, 2026
