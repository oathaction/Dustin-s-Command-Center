The atlas’s eleven routes to change, in its order, as of October 15, 2026

“A reform can travel through eleven routes, from a city council to a constitutional amendment.” Each card prints two dates: when we read the route’s official sources, and, for each reform entry, the atlas’s own check date or “Not checked”.

Feasibility, the atlas’s four words for 2026 to 2030

  • High: “The route is open, and the reform has been adopted somewhere through it since 2020.”
  • Medium: “The route is open in some places, and a credible campaign could carry the reform through it by 2030.”
  • Low: “The route exists, but it needs a coalition that does not exist now.”
  • Very low: “It needs a supermajority, a constitutional change or a reversal of court doctrine.”

What a route depends on, the atlas’s five questions

  • Authority: “Who has the legal power to adopt the change, and what courts have said about that power.”
  • Funding: “What it costs, who pays, and whether the money recurs each year.”
  • Enforcement: “Who makes it stick, with what remedy, and whether that enforcer can act.”
  • Capacity: “Whether the people and systems exist to run it.”
  • Durability: “How easily the next legislature, administration, court or ballot measure can undo it.”

  1. Route LOC“Local”

    Ordinance, charter amendment or local practice.

    Who acts City councils, county boards, charter commissions, voters in home-rule places

    Its stages

    1. Home-rule power, within the state’s limits
    2. Introduced and sent to a committee
    3. Hearing
    4. Two or three readings
    5. Council vote
    6. The mayor signs or vetoes
    7. Override by two-thirds
    8. In force, or sent to a referendum

    Portland, Oregon’s charter gives its mayor no veto.

    Official sources, read November 11 and 14, 2026: Massachusetts Constitution, Amendment Article LXXXIX; the charters of Boston, Baltimore and Portland, Oregon; the Minneapolis City Council’s rules

    15 reform entries name this route; the atlas checked two of them, on October 15, 2026.

    What it depends on, and its 15 entries

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “State home-rule law and preemption, a higher government limiting what a lower one may do. In Dillon’s Rule states, where cities hold only the powers the state grants, the legislature can override local choices at will; 19 states prohibit ranked-choice voting in some or all elections.”

  2. Route SS“State statute”

    A bill passed by the legislature and signed, or a rule issued by a state agency or utility commission under a statute.

    Who acts Legislators, governors, state agencies

    Its stages

    1. Introduced and sent to a committee
    2. Hearing and report
    3. Readings and a vote in each chamber
    4. The governor’s days to act
    5. Veto, item veto and override
    6. A bill left at adjournment

    Nebraska’s Legislature has one chamber.

    Official sources, read November 11 and 14, 2026: the constitutions of Massachusetts, Nebraska, Virginia and Arizona; the Massachusetts Joint Rules and the Rules of the Nebraska Legislature

  3. Route BI“State ballot”

    Citizen initiative, referendum or legislative referral.

    Who acts Voters, petition campaigns, courts that review ballot language

    Its stages

    1. Filed
    2. Title and summary
    3. Signatures
    4. The Legislature votes first, in an indirect initiative
    5. On the ballot
    6. Vote
    7. Certified, then in effect
    8. Amended or repealed later, as each state allows

    Official sources, read November 14, 2026: the constitutions of Massachusetts, Ohio and Michigan; the California Secretary of State’s Statewide Initiative Guide

  4. Route FS“Federal statute”

    Act of Congress, including appropriations and reconciliation.

    Who acts House, Senate, President

    Its stages

    1. Introduced and sent to committee
    2. Passed by each chamber, by the usual rules, suspension, cloture or reconciliation
    3. One text
    4. Presented to the President
    5. Signed, vetoed and overridden, or pocket vetoed
    6. Public law
    7. Funded, put to work by rules, reviewed by Congress and the courts
    8. Ended by a sunset, a court or a repeal

    Rule 15.22 draws all its stages, each with its source.

    Official sources, read November 11 and 14, 2026: the U.S. Constitution, Article I; House Rule XV and the House’s and Senate’s own pages; 2 U.S.C. 641 and 683, and 5 U.S.C. 553, 706 and 801

  5. Route FR“Chamber rule or confirmation”

    House or Senate rule; Senate confirmation of nominees.

    Who acts A majority of one chamber

    Its stages

    1. Each chamber sets its own rules
    2. The House adopts its rules each Congress, and the Senate’s carry over
    3. A Senate rule changes by two-thirds, or by a precedent set by majority vote
    4. Nomination
    5. Committee
    6. The Senate’s vote to advise and consent

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: the U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 5, and Article II, Section 2; the Standing Rules of the Senate; the Senate’s roll call votes of November 21, 2013 and April 6, 2017; House Resolution 5 of the 119th Congress

  6. Route FA“Federal executive action”

    Executive order, agency rule, guidance, staffing and budget execution.

    Who acts President, agency heads

    Its stages

    1. Executive order, published in the Federal Register
    2. White House review of a draft rule
    3. Proposed rule, comment and final rule
    4. Guidance, with no comment period
    5. Report to Congress, and disapproval
    6. Court review
    7. Revoked by a later order

    What one order does, another can undo.

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: Executive Order 12866 and 44 U.S.C. 1505; 5 U.S.C. 551, 553, 706, 801 and 802; Executive Orders 13992 and 14148

    7 reform entries name this route; the atlas checked one of them, on October 15, 2026.

    What it depends on, and its seven entries

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “Authority granted by statute, and the next administration. This is the least durable route: what one order does, another can undo.”

  7. Route ENF“Enforcement and litigation”

    Cases and settlements under existing law.

    Who acts The Justice Department, the Federal Trade Commission, the National Labor Relations Board, state attorneys general and private plaintiffs

    Its stages

    1. Complaint
    2. Discovery, settlement or trial
    3. An agency’s own complaint
    4. Consent order or judgment, open for public comment
    5. A court’s finding that a settlement serves the public interest
    6. Appeal

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: 15 U.S.C. 16 and 45, 16 CFR 2.34 and 29 U.S.C. 160; U.S. Courts, “Civil Cases” and “Appeals”; the National Labor Relations Board, “Investigate Charges”

    5 reform entries name this route; the atlas checked two of them, on October 15, 2026.

    What it depends on, and its five entries

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “An agency with a quorum, staff and budget, and the courts. Consent decrees bind for a set term; verdicts can be appealed for years.”

  8. Route CRT“Court doctrine”

    Rulings that change what other routes may do.

    Who acts Federal and state courts

    Its stages

    1. District court
    2. Appeal from a final decision
    3. Decision, rehearing or remand
    4. Petition to the Supreme Court
    5. The Court chooses whether to hear it
    6. A later ruling can replace earlier doctrine

    Official sources, read November 12 and 14, 2026: 28 U.S.C. 1254, 1291, 2101 and 2106; the Rules of the Supreme Court; U.S. Courts, “Court Role and Structure” and “Appeals”; Loper Bright Enterprises v. Raimondo, 2024

  9. Route AV“Article V”

    Constitutional amendment.

    Who acts Two-thirds of each house of Congress and three-quarters of the states, or a convention

    Its stages

    1. Proposed by two-thirds of both houses, or by a convention two-thirds of the states call
    2. Ratified by three-fourths of the states, 38 of 50
    3. Certified by the Archivist

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: the U.S. Constitution, Article V; 1 U.S.C. 106b and the National Archives

    3 reform entries name this route, and the atlas checked none of them.

    What it depends on, and its three entries

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “Supermajorities across parties and regions. Treated as a long-horizon option.”

  10. Route IC“Interstate compact”

    Matching statutes in several states that take effect at a threshold.

    Who acts State legislatures

    Its stages

    1. Enacted state by state
    2. In effect at its threshold
    3. Consent of Congress, where a court requires it
    4. Withdrawal

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: the U.S. Constitution, Article I, Section 10, and Virginia v. Tennessee, 1893; Washington’s and Minnesota’s statutes for the national popular vote compact

    One reform entry names this route, and the atlas has not checked it.

    What it depends on, and its entry

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “Enough states joining to reach the threshold, and courts accepting the compact without the consent of Congress where that question is disputed.”

    Its entry, as of October 15, 2026

    • Reform R-50“Popular-vote compact and constitutional amendment options”Route ICLow feasibility. Not checked

  11. Route CIV“Civil society”

    Organizing, philanthropy, newsrooms, unions, membership groups, private contracts.

    Who acts Nonprofits, funders, workers, citizens

    Its stages

    1. Organize, assemble and petition
    2. Fund a charity, which may not campaign
    3. Fund an advocacy group
    4. Bargain as workers

    No government stage decides this route; the law sets its limits.

    Official sources, read November 12, 2026: the U.S. Constitution, First Amendment; the Internal Revenue Service’s pages on 501(c)(3) and 501(c)(4) groups; 29 U.S.C. 157

    4 reform entries name this route, and the atlas checked none of them.

    What it depends on, and its four entries

    Depends on, in the atlas’s words: “Money and members. No government action is needed, which makes it the fastest route and the easiest to underfund.”
